A B S T R A C T
Over 53 % of African road network is unpaved, yet systematic monitoring remains limited. This study introduces
a cost-effective machine learning (ML) solution to help local authorities monitor and plan road maintenance.
Building on earlier work using high-resolution satellite imagery in Tanzania, the analysis extends to Madagascar,
incorporating medium- and low-resolution imagery to reduce costs. Two distinct methodologies were evaluated:
traditional ML and multimodal ML. The multimodal ML model achieves 93.2 % accuracy with high-resolution
imagery and maintains satisfactory performance with medium (84.0 %) and low-resolution (85.3 %) imagery,
aided by transfer learning. The framework demonstrates robust cross-resolution performance across Tanzania
and Madagascar contexts. Additionally, a pilot study explored a fine-tuned Large Language-and-Vision Assistant
(LLaVA) model, which demonstrated potential for natural language-based condition reporting and maintenance
recommendations, offering an interpretable alternative to quantitative classification outputs. Whilst LLaVA
currently exhibits lower classification accuracy than the multimodal ML model, multi-turn conversational ap-
proaches show promise for enhancing performance whilst maintaining natural language interpretability. This
study contributes to Sustainable Development Goal 9.1 by delivering a scalable, affordable strategy to support
resilient infrastructure and economic development in low-income regions.
1. Introduction
Road infrastructure is fundamental for accessing essential services
and fostering economic growth, yet a substantial proportion of networks
in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) remains unpaved. In Af-
rica, 53 % of roads are unpaved, a figure unlikely to decline in the near
term (African Development Bank Group, 2014). Tanzania typifies this
challenge: only 7 % of the 180,000 km road network is paved (PADECO
Co. Ltd., 2012). Consequently, over one billion rural residents world-
wide, including 59 % of Africa's population (Mikou et al., 2019), rely on
all-weather access routes that are frequently impassable (World Bank,
2016). Strengthening these vital links is therefore critical for poverty
reduction and inclusive growth.
Maintaining these networks in LMICs is restricted by scarce budgets,
logistical hurdles, and environmental stressors. Gravel and other ag-
gregates are often inaccessible or unaffordable, while intensifying
rainfall and extreme weather accelerate surface degradation (Harral and
Faiz, 1988; Workman et al., 2023). Reliable, up-to-date road condition
data are crucial for efficient resource allocation, but national inventories
are frequently outdated, inconsistent, or incomplete. Conventional
condition surveys, involving driven surveys and visual inspections, are
labour-intensive, costly, subjective, and sporadically implemented. A

Whilst substantial progress has been achieved in paved road assess-
ment using CNN-based models (Cadamuro et al., 2018; Jiang et al.,
2021) and UAV-based approaches for specific applications such as
measuring road deformation caused by landslides (Cardenal et al.,
2019), considerably less attention has been directed towards unpaved
road networks. Oppong Adu et al. (2023) evaluated unpaved road
conditions using UAV imagery and traditional field surveys, highlighting
that whilst UAVs offer a cost-effective alternative, they require greater
automation to optimise utility. Building on earlier UAV-based work by
Lopes Amaral Loures (2021) applying CNNs with drone imagery to
evaluate unpaved roads in Canada, Loures & Azar (2023) developed
enhanced computer vision solutions that automatically identify com-
mon unpaved road distresses (potholes, rutting, corrugation) using
modified YOLOv3 (precision 91.95 %, recall 89.31 %) and estimate
pothole dimensions from drone video frames through semantic seg-
mentation with mean absolute error of 10.8 cm, demonstrating cost-
effective monitoring for remote communities. However, scalability re-
mains constrained by operational requirements such as trained opera-
tors, visual line-of-sight regulations, and multiple launch sites for
comprehensive coverage.
Satellite-based approaches offer greater scalability potential for un-
paved road networks. In 2016, TRL conducted studies in rural areas
across Ghana, Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda, and Zambia using high-
resolution imagery, where trained staff manually interpreted road con-
ditions based on indicators such as pixel intensity and road width
(Workman et al., 2018). Accuracy ranged from 63 % for five-category
classification (Excellent/Good/Fair/Poor/Bad) to 85 % for simplified
three-category classification (Good/Fair/Poor), though this method re-
mains subjective and depends on the experience and knowledge of in-
dividual assessor. In pursuit of more objective solutions, advanced trials
in the Kilosa district of Tanzania demonstrated that integrating satellite
imagery (0.3–0.5 m resolution) with ML could achieve up to 88 % ac-
curacy in rural road condition assessment (Workman et al., 2023). By
decoding pixel-level variations in imagery, this approach effectively
identifies areas indicative of deterioration. This method is particularly
suited for high-level assessments at Information Quality Levels (IQL) 4
and 5 and proves advantageous in remote or conflict-affected areas
where traditional surveys are impractical or unsafe. Moreover, this
method allows for simultaneous evaluation of extensive road networks,
whereas conventional surveys may take months or even years to com-
plete. Despite these promising results, widespread adoption remains
constrained by persistent barriers. The high cost of high-resolution im-
agery restricts deployment in budget-limited LMICs, whilst the absence
of comprehensive software frameworks hinders scalable implementa-
tion. Critically, trials employing lower-cost imagery alternatives have
yielded only 56 % accuracy, falling short of operational requirements

2. Satellite imagery collection and ground truthing
2.1. Satellite imagery collection
In September 2023, satellite imagery at three distinct resolutions was
acquired for the AoI in Madagascar through the UP42 platform from
three providers: (1) Pleiades Neo (Airbus Defence and Space) providing
0.3 m high-resolution imagery via four-band multispectral sensor (Blue:
450–520 nm, Green: 530–590 nm, Red: 620–690 nm, Near-Infrared:
770–880 nm) with 12-bit radiometric resolution and native multispec-
tral resolution of 1.2 m pansharpened to 0.3 m (Cantrell et al., 2023); (2)
Satellogic NewSat providing 0.7 m medium-resolution imagery from
multispectral sensors with four RGB bands plus near-infrared (750–900
nm), enhanced from 0.99 m native resolution through proprietary super-
resolution processing with 10-bit radiometric depth (Vrabel et al.,
2022); and (3) Vision-1 (OHB System AG/Airbus) providing 0.87 m low-
resolution imagery with four-band multispectral sensor (Blue: 440–510
nm, Green: 510–590 nm, Red: 600–670 nm, Near-Infrared: 780–910 nm)
with 10-bit radiometric resolution (Vrabel et al., 2024). All imagery was
acquired with near-nadir viewing geometry (off-nadir angles <15◦) to
minimize geometric distortion. All imagery was delivered pansharpened
(combining multispectral and panchromatic bands for enhanced spatial
resolution) and orthorectified to remove terrain distortions, with
radiometric calibration to top-of-atmosphere reflectance applied by
suppliers using sensor-specific calibration coefficients derived from on-
orbit vicarious calibration and laboratory characterization. No addi-
tional atmospheric correction was performed, as comparative analysis
across resolutions benefited from consistent radiometric processing
levels. The imagery covered approximately 125 km2 and reflected a
wide spectrum of unpaved road conditions, ranging from well-
maintained to severely deteriorated. The acquisition was scheduled to
coincide with ground-truthing activities, ensuring that satellite imagery
analysis was synchronised with on-the-ground assessments to enhance
data accuracy. Fig. 1 illustrates the satellite imagery at the three spatial
resolutions used.
2.2. Ground truthing
Ground truthing links satellite-derived information to on-site ob-
servations and is crucial for monitoring dynamic road conditions in
contexts such as Madagascar, where unpaved roads deteriorate rapidly
due to environmental factors and high usage. Validating satellite im-
agery against real-world data also provides the empirical basis for
training and testing machine learning (ML) models. The ground-truthing
activities were planned and executed within six weeks of satellite image
acquisition to align closely with the timing of the image capture. These
activities were carried out by TRL in collaboration with the local part-
ner, ONG Lalana, who facilitated transportation and guidance on local
unpaved road assessment protocols. A total of 86 km of roads within the
AoI were surveyed.
Data collection involved capturing georeferenced high-definition
video with a dashcam (Fig. 2(a)), recording speed, average speed, and
accelerometer readings in X, Y, and Z directions. The RoadLabPro
smartphone application provided International Roughness Index (IRI)
values at 100 ± 15 m intervals (GPS-triggered), reflecting road condi-
tion severity (Sayers et al., 1986). The application was operated
following standard protocols: consistent vehicle (4 × 4 survey vehicle
with standard suspension), consistent speed (approximately 40–50 km/
h on accessible sections), and trained operator following manufacturer
guidelines. GPS horizontal accuracy was monitored, with measurements
requiring minimum 10 m horizontal accuracy for inclusion. Visual in-
spection of dashcam video footage was conducted alongside IRI mea-
surements to validate condition classifications, with IRI serving as a
quantitative supplement to visual assessment rather than the sole
determinant of road condition. It is important to acknowledge the
measurement limitations inherent in smartphone-based IRI systems
compared to Class 1 inertial profilers (ASTM International, 2023).
However, for ML training applications where condition classes span
substantial IRI ranges, this precision level remains adequate. In Mada-
gascar's road classification system, condition categories correspond to
IRI ranges established by local road authorities: Good (IRI < 10 m/km),
Fair (IRI 10–15 m/km), Poor (IRI 15–20 m/km), and Bad (IRI > 20 m/
km). The measurement uncertainty of smartphone-based IRI systems
(typically ±0.5–1.0 m/km based on validation studies (Ekpenyong
et al., 2021) is substantially smaller than these 5 m/km class boundaries,
ensuring that measurement noise does not introduce systematic label-
ling errors in training data. Quality assurance was maintained through:
(1) real-time monitoring of GPS accuracy and sensor status, with
anomalous readings flagged for repeat measurement where operation-
ally feasible; (2) cross-validation of IRI-based classifications against vi-
sual inspection by local road engineering specialists from ONG Lalana
and L'Autorit´e Routi`ere de Madagascar, who are familiar with Mada-
gascar's road condition assessment protocols; and (3) removal of mea-
surements with insufficient GPS accuracy (>15 m horizontal error) that
could spatially misalign IRI values with satellite imagery tiles. These
protocols provided ground truth data suitable for supervised ML model
training in resource-constrained field settings, balancing measurement
quality with operational feasibility for an 86 km road network survey.

3.4. Data cleansing – Outliers detection
Data cleansing is essential for effective ML analysis, ensuring data-
sets are free from inaccuracies and inconsistencies that may skew re-
sults. In this study, outliers are defined as image tiles containing
occlusions (e.g., vehicles, vegetation, shadows) or significantly devi-
ating road surface conditions within their classification categories. Such
anomalies could distort statistical measures and degrade ML perfor-
mance (Ur Rehman and Belhaouari, 2021).
Outlier detection typically employs two approaches: univariate and
multivariate detection techniques. While univariate methods, such as
histogram analysis (μ ± 3σ criterion), boxplot examination (IQR
method), and Q-Q plots, offer valuable insights into individual feature
distributions, they can be misleading when assessing complex, high-
dimensional data (Leys et al., 2019). To address this limitation, a
multivariate approach combining Principal Component Analysis (PCA)
and Mahalanobis distance was implemented. PCA reduced the dataset
dimensionality to three principal components, after which Mahalanobis
distance identified outliers.

the Mahalanobis Distance. This method effectively segregates the data
into distinct clusters and outliers across various road condition labels.

4.1. Traditional ML models

Traditional machine learning (ML) models were developed using
PyCaret (version 3.0) (Moez Ali, 2020), an open-source, low-code

A systematic model development strategy was employed using
PyCaret's capabilities for comparing, tuning, and blending algorithms:
1. Data Preparation: The dataset was partitioned into 80 % for
training and validation (with 10-fold cross-validation) and 20 % for
testing on unseen data, ensuring robust model evaluation
2. Model Comparison and Selection: Multiple algorithms were eval-
uated using `compare_models()` to identify top performers.
3. Ensemble Methods: The top five models were blended (`blend_-
models()`) to enhance robustness and reduce overfitting.
4. Hyperparameter Tuning: Individual top models and the blended
ensemble were fine-tuned (`tune_model()`) to optimise hyper-
parameters for the specific datasets.
5. Finalisation: The best-performing tuned ensemble model was
finalised (`finalize_model()`) for deployment.
By following this structured approach, three ensemble models were
developed for unpaved road condition assessment:
• Base_Model: Trained on the colour and basic texture dataset.
• Extended_Model: Trained on colour, basic and extended texture
features.
• PCA_Model: Trained on the feature dataset derived through the
VGG16 model, and dimensional reduced by using PCA.

PBIAS = −1.033 %) and the Extended Model (MBE = −0.039, PBIAS =

error magnitude. The explanation lies in the difference between
continuous error minimisation and discrete classification. RMSE re-
wards predictions that are numerically close to targets even when they
cross class boundaries; bias metrics expose directional error. Dimen-
sionality reduction in the PCA Model produces smoother, mean-centred
predictions that compress the dynamic range; this reduces RMSE but
increases systematic bias and weakens boundary discrimination.
Confusion matrices reflect this behaviour: the PCA Model makes 738
adjacent-class errors (Poor ↔Fair), compared with 479 and 387 for the

achieves low numerical error at the expense of boundary precision,
consistent with its weaker classification metrics (Accuracy = 71.7 %,
Kappa = 0.502, MCC =0.503). RMSE alone therefore provides an
incomplete assessment for multi-class classification; comprehensive
evaluation should include accuracy, Kappa, MCC, and bias indicators
(MBE, PBIAS) to capture both the magnitude and the direction of pre-
dictive deviation.

4.2. Multimodal ML models
Traditional ML models for unpaved road condition analysis require
resolution-specific training, separate models for high-resolution (HR:
0.3 m/pixel) and lower-resolution (LR: 0.7–0.87 m/pixel) satellite im-
agery. This approach introduces two key issues:
• Redundancy: Duplicate training pipelines for each resolution.
• Accuracy Degradation: LR models exhibit lower accuracy due to lost
spatial details.
To address these limitations, a unified multimodal model is pro-
posed. This model generalises across resolutions via transfer learning
and heterogeneous data fusion. The architecture eliminates resolution-
specific training by leveraging HR-derived features to enhance LR
inference, addressing the domain gap between HR and LR inputs.
Furthermore, the framework supports future enhancements through
integration of additional structured data (e.g., road width variation,
traffic density, weather patterns, historical maintenance records), to
further improve predictive accuracy and provide a more thorough un-
derstanding of the factors that affect road conditions. For instance,
multimodal ML can combine image and audio data for gravel road
condition evaluation (Saeed et al., 2024) and is compatible with modern
georeferenced Road Asset Management Systems (Workman et al., 2023),
which would streamline the integration of all relevant information for a
cost-effective maintenance prioritisation process.

4.2.3. Operational workflow for real-world deployment
Practical implementation of the ML-based assessment framework
comprises four primary phases:
Phase 1: Planning and Data Acquisition (Weeks 1–2). Authorities
identify target road networks and select appropriate satellite imagery
resolution based on budget constraints and required assessment preci-
sion (refer to Table 9 for cost-accuracy trade-offs). Imagery procurement
through commercial providers (e.g., Pleiades Neo, Satellogic, Vision-1)
typically requires 7–14 days for tasking and delivery depending on cli-
matic conditions, weather etc., with bulk orders potentially securing
30–50 % cost reductions.
Phase 2: Ground Truth Collection (Weeks 2–4). Concurrent with
imagery acquisition, field teams carry out validation surveys on repre-
sentative road sections (typically 15–20 % of network length) using vi-
sual inspections or measured assessments in accordance with the
existing condition criteria and guidelines of the Roads Authority. In this
study, road condition data were collected and measured using dashcam
video, IRI measurements, and georeferencing equipment, as detailed in
Section 2.2. This validation data serves dual purposes: calibrating model
predictions to local road characteristics and providing quality assurance
benchmarks.
Phase 3: Model Application and Analysis (Week 5). Acquired
imagery undergoes automated processing: tile extraction, feature
computation, and classification using pre-trained models. The multi-
modal ML framework requires minimal local computational resources
(standard desktop with GPU), with processing rates of approximately
500–800 km of road network per day, substantially faster than con-
ventional survey rates (20–40 km per day).
Phase 4: Reporting and Maintenance Planning (Week 6). Clas-
sification results are integrated into geospatial platforms, generating
condition maps, prioritisation matrices, and budget allocation recom-
mendations. Outputs align with established road asset management
systems, facilitating integration into existing institutional workflows.

4.3. Cost-effectiveness analysis
4.3.1. Cost effectiveness methodology
This section presents a preliminary cost-benefit analysis comparing
ML-based and conventional survey methods for assessing unpaved road
conditions in Madagascar. The justification for this analysis is supported
by the demonstrated accuracy of the ML model, which achieved 93.2 %
with high-resolution imagery and at least 84 % with lower-resolution
imagery. These accuracy levels exceed or are comparable to those
attained by trained local technical engineers (Workman et al., 2016).
The primary objective is to offer an initial comparison of the financial
and operational implications of both approaches, highlighting the po-
tential cost savings and efficiency gains associated with adopting ML-
based methods for large-scale road condition monitoring.
The analysis draws on cost data collected during the implementation
phase of this study in September 2023, providing a grounded compari-
son based on real-world expenditures. While both methods offer distinct
advantages, the ML approach, which leverages satellite-based assess-
ments, allows for extensive geographic coverage and is less vulnerable to
disruptions from conflicts or other logistical challenges. Moreover, it
provides objective, consistent results without the need for continuous
human intervention. On the other hand, conventional surveys can detect
surface defects, record drainage status, and potentially measure gravel
loss. However, conventional surveys face challenges like limited access
to vehicles, logistical difficulties, and inconsistent training, which can
lead to irregular and potentially unreliable data collection.
It is important to note that this analysis is not intended to definitively
determine the superiority of one method over the other. Instead, it
serves as a starting point for understanding the potential economic im-
plications of transitioning to an ML-based road condition assessment.
While the ML approach shows promise in terms of cost-efficiency and
scalability, more comprehensive studies, which account for a broader
range of economic factors, such as long-term maintenance costs, data
accuracy, and sustainability, are necessary to make a more conclusive
comparison between the two methods.
4.3.1.1. Conventional survey cost framework. Conventional surveys of
unpaved road conditions typically involve in-person inspection, where
field teams drive along selected routes to document road conditions.
These efforts require a combination of labour for data collection and
technical staff for data analysis. Associated costs can be categorised as
follows:
• Labour cost: Expenses for surveyors, data processors, and support
staff.
• Vehicle and Equipment Costs: Costs for vehicles and any speci-
alised data collection tools.
• Operational Costs: Fuel, maintenance, and logistical expenditures.
• Data Analysis Costs: Expenses for processing and interpreting
collected data, including software tools.
• Time Costs: Extended project durations due to slow data acquisition
can increase total costs.
4.3.1.2. ML survey cost framework. ML-based surveys involve two pri-
mary cost components: satellite imagery acquisition and model appli-
cation. Among these, imagery acquisition represents the most significant
expenditure. The total imagery cost is determined by the length and
density of the surveyed road network. For this analysis, the required
imagery area is calculated by multiplying the road network length by a
standard swath of 5 km, which is the minimum purchasable width, and
applying a 30 % reduction to account for overlapping areas at
intersections.

model, where users pose questions, and GPT provides answers. Each
entry in the dataset includes metadata: a unique identifier (‘id’), the file

structured dialogues were developed to guide the model in accurately
interpreting road surface conditions.

4.4.2.2. Classification performance analysis. Table 10 summarises per-
formance metrics across different data types and conversational settings,
while Fig. 14 displays the confusion matrices derived from answers_
{CONDITION}.json files. Notably, single-conversation models show a
strong prediction bias. In the binary setting (Fig. 14 (a)), the model
predicts the Bad/Poor class with a 93 % true positive rate but mis-
classifies 96 % of Fair/Good samples. Similarly, in the four-category
setting (Fig. 14 (c)), it overwhelmingly predicts the Poor class. This
skewed behaviour is likely attributed to the limited resolution of the
input images, which fails to capture subtle differences in surface con-
ditions. Another consideration is the original LLaVA model may asso-
ciate unpaved roads with rural or dirt roads (unpaved roads are typically
categorised as gravel, or earth/dirt roads), which are typically classified
as Poor or Bad compared to paved roads. This inherent bias in the pre-
trained model could contribute to the observed preference for predict-
ing Poor or Bad road conditions.
In contrast, the multi-conversation setting demonstrates improved
generalization. In 2-category multi-conversion (Fig. 14 (b)), the true
positive rates for Bad/Poor and Fair/Good are 89 % and 82 %, respec-
tively. This improved performance indicates that the multi-conversion
approach allows the model to generalise better across categories by
leveraging additional augmented feature representations. However, the
misclassification rate for Bad/Poor increases from 7 % (in single con-
version) to 11 %, suggesting that while the model better identifies Fair/
Good, it struggles slightly more with differentiating ambiguous Bad/
Poor cases. In 4-Category Multi-Conversion (Fig. 14 (d)), the model
improves balance, achieving true positive rates of 78 % and 75 % for
Poor and Fair, respectively. Despite this improvement, the Good and Bad
classes continue to struggle with a low true positive rate of 12 % and 46
%, respectively. These results indicate that using multi-conversion can
mitigate issues caused by low images resolution. However, the dataset
exhibits severe class imbalance, with the Poor and Fair categories being
approximately 15 times larger than the Bad and Good categories. This
imbalance skews the model's learning process, leading it to prioritise the
larger classes to minimize overall prediction error.

4.4.3. Recommendations for future enhancement
The preliminary result of LLaVA for assessing unpaved road condi-
tions has shown substantial potential. Initial experiments conducted on
a single GTX 4090 GPU demonstrated that fine-tuning the model on a
multi-turn conversational dataset over 10 epochs requires approxi-
mately 16 days, with an additional day needed for evaluation. This
significant computational demand restricts conducting more advanced
and complex processing. Despite these constraints, the results offer a
clear pathway for further optimisation.
Fine-tuning with multi-turn conversations led to substantial im-
provements in model performance compared to single-turn approaches,
which exhibit poor classification accuracy. Error analysis reveals that in
multi-turn approaches, errors predominantly occur in the first conver-
sation turn where LLaVA attempts to generate visual descriptions from
raw satellite imagery. When accurate condition descriptions are pro-
vided in the second conversation turn, the model achieves 99.9 % ac-
curacy in subsequent classification. This demonstrates that LLaVA excels
at reasoning and classification when given precise feature descriptions
but struggles with direct visual interpretation of satellite imagery.
Based on this finding, enhancement pathways are proposed through
developing
task-specific
instruction-following datasets
that
target
improving the first conversation turn.

passing three context types: (1) segmentation annotations for pixel-level
defect identification, (2) quantitative colour and texture metrics derived
from RGB/HSV colour spaces and GLCM analysis, and (3) detailed
condition descriptions with tailored maintenance recommendations.
Training or fine-tuning LLaVA with such datasets could enable VLMs to
achieve both high classification accuracy and interpretable natural

Beyond improving visual description generation, addressing the
observed class imbalance represents a complementary enhancement
priority. Systematic implementation of several strategies is recom-
mended: (1) data augmentation techniques specifically targeting mi-
nority

and

class representation by 3–5 times; (2) weighted cross-entropy loss

classifications (e.g., weight ratios of 15:1 for ‘Good’/’Bad’ relative to
‘Poor’/’Fair’); (3) synthetic data generation through diffusion models or
generative adversarial networks creating realistic examples of under-
represented conditions; and (4) focal loss mechanisms down-weighting
easily classified examples (‘Poor’/’Fair’) whilst emphasising chal-
lenging minority class instances. Combining these approaches could
potentially improve minority class true positive rates from current levels
(12–46 %) to target ranges of 65–75 %, substantially enhancing prac-
tical utility for comprehensive condition assessment.
Implementation of these combined enhancement strategies will
necessitate substantial computational resources. The expanded dataset
size resulting from data augmentation and synthetic data generation,
combined with the complexity of multi-modal instruction-guided
learning using rich contextual annotations, requires advanced hardware
configurations (e.g., 8 × NVIDIA A100 GPUs) for efficient model
training. Through comprehensive training addressing both visual
description generation and class imbalance, VLM (i.e., LLaVA) could be
substantially improved to generate actionable insights and support
automated road condition reporting, contributing to more efficient
maintenance prioritisation and data-driven asset management.

6. Conclusion
This study presents innovative ML techniques for assessing unpaved
road surface conditions satellite imagery at varying resolutions and
pioneering the application of VLMs for this specific task. An open-source
framework integrating Earth observation and machine learning has been
developed to generate high-precision predictions of unpaved road con-
dition, offering a cost-effective alternative to traditional field in-
spections. The framework enables local authorities in LMICs to select
imagery resolutions that balance predictive accuracy and cost across
diverse geographic and economic contexts.
Validation focused exclusively on Madagascar, characterised by
humid tropical to subtropical climates with high rainfall (1000–3000
mm annually), predominantly laterite and gravel road surfaces, and
region-specific construction practices. This raises the question of gen-
eralisability to other areas. Three factors affect transferability to
climatically and geologically distinct African regions. First, arid and
semi-arid contexts (Sahel, Horn of Africa) exhibit fundamentally
different degradation mechanisms: wind-driven sand accumulation and
aeolian erosion dominate rather than rainfall-induced rutting and
washboard formation, potentially altering the visual signatures detected
by classification models. Second, regions with different surfacing ma-
terials (volcanic aggregates in East African highlands, clay-dominant
soils
in
West
African
lowlands)
require
model
recalibration
or
expanded training datasets to capture material-specific spectral and
textural characteristics. Third, seasonal variations differ substantially:
Madagascar's pronounced wet-dry cycles create temporal condition
fluctuations exceeding those in consistently arid or humid regions,
affecting optimal imagery acquisition timing. The methodological
framework (multimodal architecture, transfer learning protocols, cost-
benefit evaluation approach) transfers broadly across contexts. How-
ever, operational deployment in new geographic regions requires initial
pilot studies (100–200 km ground truth collection) to assess local model
performance and identify necessary adaptations before full-scale
implementation.
Successful deployment across diverse African contexts necessitates
strategic partnerships with local road authorities, research institutions,
and regional development organisations. Collaborative frameworks
should prioritise three key objectives: (1) establishing regional ground
truth databases that capture local road construction practices, climate
patterns, and degradation mechanisms, enabling transfer learning cali-
bration for specific geographic contexts; (2) capacity building through
training programmes that develop local expertise in satellite imagery
interpretation, ML model application, and results integration into
existing asset management systems; and (3) data sharing protocols that

region benefit neighbouring countries with similar road characteristics.
Organisations such as the African Development Bank, Regional Eco-

Pilot implementations in multiple countries (e.g., extending from

Future research should pursue several promising directions to
enhance the framework's capabilities and applicability. Technical en-
hancements include: (1) advancing VLM capabilities by developing task-
specific instruction-following datasets that incorporate rich contextual
annotations (segmentation, quantitative colour and texture metrics,
maintenance recommendations) for training or fine-tuning; (2) incor-
porating temporal dynamics through multi-date imagery analysis,
enabling detection of degradation rates and prediction of future condi-
tion trajectories to support proactive maintenance scheduling; (3)
expanding input modalities by fusing satellite imagery with weather
data (rainfall intensity, temperature cycles), traffic patterns, and soil
moisture indices to explain condition variations and enhance prediction
accuracy in heterogeneous environments; (4) developing domain
adaptation techniques enabling models trained in one geographic region
to transfer to others with minimal local ground truth, potentially
through meta-learning or few-shot learning approaches; (5) systemati-

(0.7–0.87 m), thereby bridging the performance gap between affordable
imagery and high-resolution classification accuracy through post-
acquisition computational enhancement rather than costlier sensor up-
grades. Application-focused research should examine integration with
broader infrastructure asset management systems, evaluate social equity
implications (ensuring rural and marginalised communities receive
equivalent monitoring attention), and assess framework applicability to
related infrastructure types (footpaths, drainage systems, bridges).
The key conclusions are as follows:
(1) Effectiveness of Traditional ML Models: Traditional ML models
achieved satisfactory performance with high-resolution satellite
imagery, particularly when extended feature sets were employed.
However, the effectiveness of the model declines when applied to
lower-resolution images. The similarity in performance between
the base and extended models suggests that complex feature
extraction offers limited benefit at lower resolutions and may
unnecessarily increase computational complexity.
(2) Superiority of Multimodal ML Models: Multimodal ML model,
enhanced by transfer learning, demonstrated up to 7.7 %
improvement in accuracy over traditional models. By integrating
structured feature data (e.g., colour and texture metrics and road
width) with imagery, this approach offers better assessments.
Furthermore, its flexible architecture enables the incorporation of
additional data sources, such as traffic density, weather patterns,
and
historical
maintenance
records,
potentially
further
enhancing accuracy and providing deeper insights.
(3) Robustness Across Resolutions: Traditional ML models showed
high sensitivity to image resolution, with performance losses up
to 77.3 % when applied to data of differing quality. In contrast,
the multimodal ML model maintained greater consistency across
resolutions, making it more suitable for deployment in real-
world, variable-data environments.
(4) Cost-Efficiency and Practicality: The adoption of ML tech-
niques for road assessment presents a potential cost advantages
over conventional survey methods. High-resolution imagery
analysis is estimated at €54.60 per kilometre, providing a
competitive alternative to traditional surveys while delivering
higher accuracy. Utilising lower-resolution imagery still yields
acceptable accuracy and is even more cost-effective, with rates as
low as €22.23 per kilometre. However, this cost-benefit analysis
is preliminary and based on specific data from the 2023
Madagascar study. A more comprehensive analysis that accounts
for long-term maintenance costs, variations in data accuracy, and
sustainability aspects is necessary for a conclusive comparison.
(5) Potential of Vision-Language Models (LLaVA): The fine-tuned
LLaVA-v1.5-7b model demonstrated promising capabilities in
interpreting
unpaved
road
surface
conditions
from
high-
resolution imagery. While its classification accuracy was lower
than the best-performing ML models, this was largely due to
limited training scope. LLaVA exhibited strong potential for
generating detailed and context-rich assessments when provided
with structured prompts. With further fine-tuning using task-
specific, instruction-following datasets, LLaVA could become a
valuable
tool
for
automated
reporting
and
infrastructure
evaluation.
