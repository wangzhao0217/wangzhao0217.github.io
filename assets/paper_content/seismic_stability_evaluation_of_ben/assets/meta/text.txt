A B S T R A C T
Seismic stability of rock slopes is a critical issue in geotechnical engineering, particularly in seismically active
regions where benched bedding slopes are susceptible to dynamic failure. This study explores the dynamic
response and progressive damage mechanisms of benched bedding rock slopes under seismic excitation through
shaking table tests and numerical simulations. Specifically, four slope models with varying bench widths are
analyzed, focusing on key factors such as acceleration amplification coefficients (AAC), earth pressure distri-
bution, and failure modes. The results reveal that AACs at the internal corners of benches are lower than those at
the external corners. Moreover, it is observed that slope stability improves as the bench width increases, as
evidenced by a reduction in failure occurrences for slopes with uniform bench widths of 2 m, 4 m, and 6 m. In
contrast, the slope with uneven bench widths exhibits more severe failure. Furthermore, numerical simulations
confirm that slopes with wider platforms at lower elevations are more prone to bench-scale failure. To quantify
these effects, a novel dynamic stability equation, Sqt, validated by test data, classifies stability into four levels: Sqt
< 1.0 (unstable), 1.0 < Sqt < 1.3 (less stable), 1.3 < Sqt < 1.5 (basically stable), and Sqt > 1.5 (stable). These
findings provide a necessary basis for understanding the shattering cumulative damage mechanism and seismic
fortification of complex rock slopes, and offer valuable insights for the design of cutting slopes in high-intensity
seismic areas.
1. Introduction
In recent years, major earthquakes in China have led to catastrophic
geological disasters. Notably, the Mw7.9 Wenchuan earthquake in
Sichuan Province on May 12, 2008, triggered over 15,000 landslides and
619 debris flows, causing widespread devastation (Dong et al., 2022;
Fan et al., 2016; He et al., 2021). Among these, the Daguangbao land-
slide remains the largest earthquake-induced landslide ever recorded
globally, with an astounding volume of 742 million cubic meters. This
landslide formed a dammed lake that soared to a height of 690 m (Huang
et al., 2011; Feng et al., 2022). The Mw7.1 Yushu earthquake on July 1,
2010, resulted in over 2036 landslides in Qinghai Province, claiming the
lives of 2220 people and leaving 70 unaccounted for. Similarly, the
Lushan earthquake in Ya’an on April 20, 2013, resulted in a total of 3000
geological disasters. More recently, on September 5, 2022, a magnitude
of Mw 6.8 earthquake struck Luding County in Garze Prefecture, Sichuan
Province, initiating 565 geological hazards in Luding and Shimian
counties. These included 331 collapses and 234 landslides, and

enhanced stability assessments of bench slopes in seismically active

Recent research has led to substantial advancements in both meth-
odological approaches and mechanistic understanding of slope dynamic
response and stability. With regard to dynamic response mechanisms,
Jafarzadeh et al. (2015) systematically investigated the generation of
Rayleigh waves in terrain amplification effects through a combination of
shaking table tests and ABAQUS finite element simulations. Their find-
ings quantitatively demonstrated the topographic amplification can
significantly increase induced accelerations at the slope surface,
particularly at the crest. Furthermore, they introduced the concept of the
“effective depth of surficial amplification,” providing new insight into
the influence of low-frequency seismic waves on deep slope responses.
Song et al. (2019) employed a methodological framework integrating
three-dimensional finite element modal analysis with shaking table
testing to identify, for the first time, the typical vibration modes (shear,
torsional, and flexural) and associated characteristic frequencies (12–29
Hz) of complex-structured rock slopes. Their results indicated that high-
frequency components tend to cause localized surface damage, whereas
low-frequency components are more likely to initiate progressive overall
sliding failures. In terms of the geological structure’s role in disaster
control, Yang et al. (2025a) conducted shaking table tests based on a
comparison of different weak interlayer designs (homogeneous weak
layers and those with discontinuous interfaces). Using digital image
correlation (DIC) full-field strain monitoring technology, they revealed
the key influence of the development degree of weak interlayer struc-
tures on peak ground acceleration (PGA) distribution: continuous weak
layers significantly increase PGA at the slope crest, aggravating back-
edge tension cracking, while discontinuous interfaces suppress the
amplification effect at the slope crest but lead to shear stress concen-
tration at the slope toe. Another study on anti-dip bench rock slopes
(Yang et al., 2025b) used large-scale shaking table tests and numerical
simulations to find that the bench geometry parameters (width and slope
angle) regulate the failure mode by altering the stress transfer path. The
widest bench effectively blocks internal deformation of the slope, and
the displacement baseline offset ratio (DBOR) indicator was introduced
for the first time to quantify the plastic zone expansion process.
Regarding innovations in stability assessment methods, Maheshwari and
Das (2023) established a small-scale physical model and PLAXIS HS
small model coupling platform to accurately quantify the sensitivity of
seismic settlement to slope angle and base edge distance. Pradhan and
Siddique (2020) employed the Phase2D finite element platform, inte-
grating the nonlinear Generalized Hoek-Brown (GHB) criterion and the
Geological Strength Index (GSI) to establish a classification system for

Despite these advances, three key limitations persist in the existing
literature: 1) Object limitation: Current studies predominantly address
homogeneous slopes or single-bench configurations, with limited
investigation into the seismic response transmission mechanisms and
overall system stability of benched rock slopes; 2) Methodological gap:
Conventional static indicators (such as FoS) fail to capture dynamic
cumulative damage. Although dynamic indicators like DBOR have been
proposed, no unified assessment framework integrating geological
structures, geometric morphology, and dynamic parameters has been
established; 3) Engineering applicability: Existing models lack quanti-
tative analysis of the synergistic effect of benched geometric parameters
(overall slope angle α, bench angle β, and inter-ramp angle θ), thereby
limiting
their practical utility in dynamic control during slope
construction.
This research examines the dynamic response and stability of slopes
with varying topographies using a series of shaking table tests. It ana-
lyzes acceleration and earth pressure to evaluate the amplification ef-
fects and distribution of these factors at different platform positions of
benched rock slopes. Moreover, the study also investigates the geometric
characteristics of multi-bench slopes and establishes relationships be-
tween the overall slope angle (α), bench angle (β), and inter-ramp angle
(θ). The equation for calculating the slope stability, based on geological
conditions (e.g., dip of structural surfaces), is proposed. The equation’s
applicability is verified through shaking table tests and numerical sim-
ulations. A dynamic correction coefficient (ζ) is introduced to refine
slope stability analysis, and a new stability judgment equation incor-
porating this coefficient is developed. This study fills a gap in large-scale
rock slope stability assessment, particularly regarding dynamic response
analysis and slope angle adjustment. The proposed dynamic stability
judgment equation offers a more accurate representation of the rock
mass’s mechanical behavior, providing geotechnical engineers with a
precise and practical tool for real-time slope stability analysis and ad-
justments during construction, with significant engineering application
potential.
2. Geological setting of the study slope
The study area is located in the Dongtai fold belt in Yunnan Province,
China, known for its complex geological structures and well-developed
faults. The project site is situated near the boundary between the
Sichuan-Yunnan and South China fault blocks, traversing the active
Xiaojiang fault zone. This zone, characterized by strong tectonic activity
with features of inheritance and multi-stage reactivation, has a signifi-
cant impact on the proposed road construction. The Xiaojiang fault, a
northeast-trending Holocene active fault, features a left-lateral strike-
slip movement and spans a structural width of 20 km. The exposed
geological strata in the area include Quaternary (Q4) of the Cenozoic
era, and Lower and Middle Cambrian Series (ε1 and ε2), respectively. In
this series of shaking table tests, the cutting slope from Sanbao section to
Qingshui section (K86 + 380 to K86 + 740) was selected as the proto-
type. Drill core tests were conducted at three locations along this
segment (as shown in Fig. 1(b)). The results from borehole 1 indicate the
presence of strongly weathered siltstone, borehole 2 revealed highly
weathered sandstone, and borehole 3 displayed moderately weathered
sandstone.
Under the influence of the geological structure of the study area, the
highway cutting slope exhibits a layered structure with fully developed
structural planes. The distribution and development degree of these
structural planes, as well as their physical and mechanical properties,
play a key role in determining the deformation, strength and stability of

3. Shaking table model test
3.1. Test device
The shaking table test system employed in this research was provided
by the Seismic Simulation Shaking Table Laboratory of the State Key
Laboratory of Geohazard Prevention and Geoenvironment Protection,
Chengdu University of Technology (see Fig. 2). The shaking table
equipment, produced by SERVOTEST company, UK, includes a shaking
table measuring 6 m × 4 m (Length × Width). This table is operated by
four vertical actuators and four horizontal actuators, complemented by a
1500 L@28Mpa oil source, an oil source control system, and a pulsar
vibration input control system. The maximum load capacity of the
shaking table is 4.0 × 104 kg. The actuators allow the table to achieve a
full load horizontal and vertical acceleration of 1.5 g and 1.0 g,
respectively, with corresponding maximum strokes of 300 mm and 150
mm.
The seismic simulation shaking table system can experience wave-
form distortion due to various factors, such as insufficient connection
stiffness, high harmonics from the localized shaking on the table,
nonlinearity of the servo valve, and the interactive effects between the
specimen and the table. The degree of waveform distortion can be
measured by the distortion degree, which comprises displacement
waveform distortion and acceleration waveform distortion. The equa-
tion for calculating the distortion degree is given in Eq. (1). The
distortion of displacement waveform is assessed in the maximum
displacement range, by applying sinusoidal vibration to an inertia
specimen at 50 % ~ 80 % of the maximum displacement level. The
system undergoes testing at five different frequencies (0.1, 0.5, 1, 3, and
5 Hz), with each test lasting a minimum of five minutes. The shaking
table system is required to maintain a maximum displacement distortion
of less than 3 % for sinusoidal vibrations below 5 Hz in each degree of

container measuring 3.6 m × 1.5 m × 2.1 m (Length × Width × Height)
was designed, as depicted in Fig. 4(a). The model container was

the flexible material was established as 20 cm (Xin et al., 2019; Zhang
et al., 2018). Consequently, a 20 cm polystyrene foam board was
installed inside the model container to simulate the absorbing material
in the horizontal vibration direction. Plexiglass was also installed along
the sidewalls of the model container parallel to the excitation direction,

To ensure that the model provides an accurate representation of the
prototype, shaking table tests must adhere to similarity relationships
under both static and dynamic conditions. In this study, the Buckingham
π theorem was employed, which utilizes dimensional analysis to deter-
mine the geometric similarity ratio between the model and prototype.
Thirteen key parameters were considered for the shaking table test,
including geometric dimensions L, mass density ρ, and seismic acceler-
ation a. These were designated as the fundamental control quantities
with scaling CL = 25, Cρ = 1, and Ca = 1, respectively. The similarity
relationships and dimensional representations of the shaking table test
are detailed in Table 1.
To accurately determine the physical and mechanical parameters of
the model material, a series of direct shear tests, and uniaxial
compression tests were conducted on rock mass samples. These tests
were aimed at assessing the strength parameters and mechanical prop-
erties of the rock mass and structural planes. From the results of
orthogonal tests on the similar material ratios for the slope model, an
optimal mass ratio of 10:35:25:14:2 for gypsum, barite powder, quartz
sand, water, and glycerin were selected for the slope model rock mass.
Here, barite powder and quartz sand served as fine and coarse aggre-
gates, respectively, while gypsum and water served as binding agents,
and glycerin aided in water retention. The deformation, strength and
stability of rock slopes were governed by the distribution, degree of
development and mechanical properties of the internal structural
planes. Therefore, the careful selection and proportioning of similar
materials for structural planes are crucial for obtaining accurate test
results (Qi et al., 2022; Song et al., 2018; Xin et al., 2024b). Based on the
results of laboratory physical and mechanical tests, clay, barite powder,
and glycerol were chosen as similar materials for the structural plane,
with a mass ratio of 10:25:1. Table 2 presents the target values, actual
measured values, and the degree of similarity for physical quantities
such as density, elastic modulus, and cohesion of the slope model. The
degree of similarity is defined as the ratio between the actual measured
values from the slope model and the target values of the model.
The construction processes of the benched bedding rock slope model
were divided into three primary components: rock mass, structural
plane and base. Initially, the outline of the slope, including the positions
of the interlayer and base, was marked on the acrylic plates and affixed
inside the plexiglass model container as depicted in Fig. 5(a). For the
model base, M5 cement mortar and bricks were used to precisely control
the angle of slope rock mass and structural plane. During the filling
process, slope materials were thoroughly mixed in the designated pro-
portions and layered from bottom to top following the volume control
principle, with each layer’s filling thickness maintained at about 5 cm
(see Fig. 5(b)). The surface of each rock mass and interlayer layer was
compacted and smoothed using wooden boards to ensure consistency
throughout the construction process as shown in Fig. 5(c). The density of
each layer was verified using the ring knife method to ensure the uni-
formity of the model rock mass. Accelerometers and earth pressure cells
were strategically embedded at specific positions within the model as
per the design scheme (see Fig. 5(d)). To safeguard the functionality of
the sensors, they were encased with cling film and sealed with adhesive
tape for waterproofing. After assembly, the model was allowed to cure to
ensure the materials reached their design strength prior to testing.
Gridlines spaced at 10 cm intervals were drawn on the completed bench
slope surface and reflectors were placed at grid intersections to facilitate
the collection of displacement data during seismic activity as depicted in
Fig. 5(e). The entire construction process for the benched bedding rock
slope model is shown in Fig. 5.

3.4. Testing sequences
The shaking table test was conducted by selecting the east-west (E-
W) component of the strong earthquake recorded at Wolong Station,
near the fault in the Wenchuan earthquake, to serve as the dynamic load
input. The seismic excitation was horizontally applied in the X-direction.
The time history and Fourier spectra are shown in Figs. 7(a) and 7(b).
Spectrum analysis revealed that seismic wave energy was primarily
concentrated in the first 10 s in the time domain and 12 Hz in the fre-
quency domain (see Fig. 7(c)). To evaluate the progressive failure of the
model slope subjected to varying excitation amplitudes, six distinct
loading conditions were tested: 0.1 g, 0.2 g, 0.4 g, 0.6 g, 0.8 g, and 1.0 g.
Following each loading, the deformation and failure of the model slope
were observed and recorded at 20-min intervals. Before each excitation,
a 0.05 g white noise was also applied to obtain the natural frequency of
the model slope. The test loading scheme is illustrated in Fig. 7(d).
4. Analysis of the test results
4.1. Seismic response characteristics of slope model
4.1.1. Distribution law of slope’s acceleration
The inertial force generated by seismic acceleration is a critical factor
for slope deformation and instability under an earthquake action.
Consequently, variations in acceleration are essential for evaluating the
dynamic response of the slope (Wu et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2022).
Therefore, this section focuses on the horizontal acceleration amplifi-
cation coefficient of the benched bedding rock slope with structural
planes at different platform positions as illustrated in Fig. 8. In this
figure, legend “222” implies that the actual widths of the third, second,
and first benches of the slope are all 2 m, whereas “282” signifies that the
actual widths of the third, second, and first benches are 2 m, 8 m, and 2
m, respectively. The acceleration amplification coefficient (AAC) is
defined as the ratio of the peak horizontal acceleration at each
measuring point to the peak acceleration at the base of the shaking table.
The acceleration responses at the internal and external corners of the
first and second benches were analyzed under various excitation am-
plitudes. The results indicate that the acceleration response of all four
slope models significantly increases as the excitation amplitude rises,
particularly up to 0.6 g. Among these, the slope with 6 m uniform bench
width displays the highest acceleration values. When the excitation
amplitude ranges from 0.6 g to 0.8 g, the acceleration response at the
internal corners of the first and second benches of the slope with uneven
bench width decreases significantly (as shown in Figs. 8(a) and 8(c)).
This reduction is attributed to the extensive propagation of internal
cracks within the slope when the excitation amplitude exceeds 0.6 g.
These cracks absorb seismic wave energy, thereby reducing the moni-
tored acceleration at the internal corners.

top of the slope and reaches the maximum values at the slope top
regardless of the slope surface patterns. This trend highlights the
elevation amplification effects (EAE) of seismic-induced acceleration
responses in bedding rock slopes. The test results reveal that AACs at the

4.1.2. Distribution law of slope’s earth pressure
The stability assessment of slopes after an earthquake primarily de-
pends on understanding the stress changes induced by seismic excitation
and the subsequent stress redistribution. These factors are crucial for
determining the deformation mechanisms and damage modes of slopes
during multi-stage seismic events. For example, benched bedding slopes
experience damage due to the combined effects of dynamic and residual
earth pressures. During seismic excitation, both dynamic and residual
earth pressures develop. While dynamic earth pressure dissipates after
excitation, residual earth pressure persists, causing further deformation
until significant cracking occurs. This section analyzes the peak earth
pressure responses at the internal corners of the first and second benches
in four slope models to explore the pressure response patterns.

4.1.3. Failure process analysis
Fig. 11 illustrates the failure phenomena of four slope models sub-
jected to multi-stage seismic excitations. In the scheme 1 of tests, a slope
with 2 m uniform bench width exhibited no significant failure when the
acceleration amplitude was below 0.6 g. However, when the amplitude
increased to 0.6 g, an initial crack appeared at the internal corner of the
third bench. Under repeated tension-shearing action of seismic waves,
tensile cracks appeared at the internal corners of all benches, and
numerous tension-shearing cracks parallel to the interlayer appeared on
both sides of the structural plane near the slope surface at an accelera-
tion amplitude of 0.8 g. At this stage, the shear stress became more
concentrated at the internal corners, diminishing the shear strength and
stability of the slope. At an acceleration amplitude of 1.0 g, the surface
cracks of the slope propagated and became deeper and wider, while the
density of cracks on the side of the slope increased. From the front to the
rear edge of the slope, the density of cracks decreased successively,
indicating that the transverse shear action of seismic waves had a sig-
nificant impact on the transformation of the rock mass at the bottom.
This also implies a propensity for the slope to slide down along the
structural plane. Despite the presence of cracks throughout the slope
body under multi-stage earthquake action, no complete collapses were
observed. The pre-existing cracks dissipated a portion of the seismic
energy during the vibration excitation process, enhancing the filtering
effect of the rock mass and resulting in failure phenomena characterized
by produced cracks on the slope body without a loss of overall stability
(Liu et al., 2024; Lu, 2015; Wang et al., 2024).
In contrast, the slope with uneven bench width exhibited more se-
vere failure phenomena (as shown in Fig. 11(b)). At an excitation
acceleration amplitude of 0.6 g, initial tensile cracks appeared at the
internal corner of the second bench due to the seismic dynamics.
Concurrently, the slope was affected by the elevation amplification ef-
fects (EAE), resulting in shear cracks parallel to the structural plane’s
strike in the rock mass with high elevations on the slope, with the rock
mass tending to slide along the structural plane under seismic action.
When the acceleration amplitude reached 0.8 g, cracks at the external
corner of the second bench rotated outward from slope due to the syn-
ergistic effect of the free surface amplification and seismic inertia force,
manifesting as the propagating of cracks along the slope’s free surface in
the reciprocating vibration process. The tension cracks, generally wider
at the top and narrower at the bottom, gradually extended downward.
After multi-stage seismic excitations, some cracks interconnected with
shear cracks parallel to the interlayer strike, splitting the slopes into
fractured rock mass and increasing the wave filtering effects of the slope
bodies. At an excitation acceleration amplitude of 1.0 g, the second
wider bench tended to slide down along the structural plane under the
strong seismic inertia force, with the upper part of the slope structure
compressing the lower part repeatedly during the earthquake dynamic
action. Eventually, the first bench of the slope was completely squeezed
and slid down quickly along the structural plane and out of the slope. It
is worth noting that the fractured rock fell into the crevice, forming a
wedge splitting effect that further expanded the crack downward at the
moment of crack opening. After test loading, the rear edge of the slope
was 8 cm away from the absorption boundary, and a settlement of 9 cm
occurred, indicating that the upper structure of the slope slid along the
structural plane. The position of each platform on the slope model
showed significant misalignment compared to its initial state after the

5. Discussion
5.1. Slope deformation damage process
Shaking table tests are a crucial tool in earthquake engineering,
enabling precise simulation of seismic parameters (e.g., acceleration,
frequency, and duration) and revealing structural dynamic responses
under repeatable loading conditions (Chen et al., 2023; Yang et al.,
2022). Unlike numerical simulations, these tests allow direct observa-
tion of structural damage, providing empirical validation for theoretical
models. However, they have two main limitations: size constraints, as
large or complex structures require scaled models that may introduce
size effects or boundary distortion, and discrepancies in simulating
seismic motion, as real earthquake waves exhibit spatial non-uniformity
and multi-dimensional coupling, which are challenging to fully repli-
cate, especially with respect to randomness and local geological effects.
Despite these challenges, the shaking table used in this study (6.0 m
× 4.0 m) offers a favorable specimen-to-table mass ratio, mitigating
boundary distortion from scaled model effects. Displacement and ac-
celeration distortions in the X, Y, and Z directions remain within
acceptable limits. Therefore, the shaking table meets high precision
standards, and the data obtained are reliable and accurate.

g–0.6 g), accelerated deformation (0.6 g–0.8 g), and overall instability
(0.8 g–1.0 g). The model test results show that the deformation damage

stability must be continuously tracked and monitored. Moreover, the
stability evaluations require constant revision of the calculated condi-
tions and parameters. Therefore, the evaluation methods and means are
also undergoing dynamic development. Consequently, the dynamic
evaluation of slope is a continuous cycle process of theoretical calcula-
tion and field monitoring “interaction”.

5.2. Identification of the slope stability
To examine the impact of varying widths of wider platforms (i.e.,
different b values) and their positions on slope stability, we developed
several slope models with uneven bench widths (where a/b ∕= 1). The
parameters α, β, θ, and δ were extracted from these models, and the Sq
values for each slope model were calculated and are presented in
Table 5. Notably, when b/a = 2 and the wider platform is placed on the
third bench, the Sq value is 1.266; when b/a = 3 and the platform is on
the third bench, the Sq value is 1.003. As the b/a ratio increases, the Sq
value decreases, indicating a reduction in slope stability. The Sq distri-
bution for the 8 2 2, 2 8 2, and 2 2 8 slopes follows the trend observed in
numerical simulations. This confirms that the proposed Sq stability
equation is consistent with both model test results and numerical sim-
ulations, demonstrating its strong applicability to benched bedding rock
slopes. Additionally, as shown in Table 5, regardless of platform width, if
the platform is positioned on the first bench, the Sq value remains
around 0.2, indicating extremely poor stability. Consequently, these
slopes are highly prone to bench-scale damage at the internal corner of
the first bench. Therefore, reinforcement of these slopes, particularly at
the internal corner of the wider bench, is essential to prevent bench-
scale failure and maintain slope stability.
The natural frequency and damping ratio are critical dynamic pa-

Zhao et al., 2023). These forces cause deformation and damage to the
slope’s internal structure, leading to slope deterioration. Changes in the
natural frequency and damping ratio indicate the extent of internal
damage to the slope model. By analyzing the acceleration time history
curves, the slope’s dynamic parameters can be calculated. These pa-

The Technical code for building slope engineering (GB50011–2010) in
China outlines guidelines for slope safety classification. Section 3.2
specifies that classification should consider factors such as potential
damage severity, slope type, and height, categorizing slopes into three
safety levels: Class I, Class II, and Class III. Section 5.3 outlines the slope
stability evaluation criteria, with Subsection 5.3.2 defining the slope
stability safety factor (Fst). For slopes under “normal conditions”, the Fst
for Class I slopes is 1.35, while under “seismic conditions”, the Fst is 1.15.
For Class II slopes, the Fst under “normal conditions” is 1.30, and under
“seismic conditions”, it is 1.10. For Class III slopes, the Fst under “normal
conditions” is 1.25, and under “seismic conditions”, it is 1.05. This
standard suggests that the stability safety factor is reduced under seismic
conditions, reflecting a reduction coefficient applied to the “normal
conditions” safety factor. This concept is incorporated into the stability
equation presented in this paper. As shown by the transfer function
analysis, slope failure is a dynamic process involving cumulative dam-
age, necessitating the inclusion of a dynamic correction coefficient, ζ,
which accounts for slope displacement and crack propagation rate.
Larger displacements and faster crack propagation negatively impact
stability.
Based on the failure processes observed during shaking table tests,
and using displacement sensors, the displacement of slopes during
seismic excitation was measured to determine the dynamic correction
coefficient for each slope. The equation for evaluating dynamic stability,
incorporating the dynamic correction coefficient, is proposed, with ζ
values provided in Table 7.
The Code for investigation of geotechnical engineering (GB50021–2001)
of China specifies that for newly designed slopes, the stability coefficient
K should range from 1.30 to 1.50 for important projects, 1.15 to 1.30 for
general projects, and 1.05 to 1.15 for minor projects. Similarly, the
Technical code for building slope engineering (GB50330–2013) of China
stipulates that for slope engineering stability verification, the stability
coefficient should be no less than the safety factor requirement Ks
(ranging from 1.20 to 1.35) to avoid necessitating slope reinforcement.
In Queensland, Australia, Geotechnical Design Standard–Minimum Re-
quirements states that stability analysis for geotechnical design of a
cutting slope must adhere to a minimum factor of safety (FOS) of 1.30
during construction (short-term). This study classifies slope stability
based on the dynamic stability equation, Sqt (as shown in Fig. 19). The
dynamic stability is considered basically stable if Sqt falls within the
range of 1.3 to 1.50, with potential for localized damage. When Sqt ex-
ceeds 1.5, the slope is deemed fully stable. A Sqt value between 1.0 and
1.3 indicates less stable, while a value below 1.0 suggests very poor
stability and a high likelihood of bench-scale failure. Consequently, the
dynamic stability Sqt of slopes should not be lower than 1.0. The dy-
namic stability equation provides geotechnical engineers and rock en-
gineers with a practical means to assess the dynamic stability of multi-
bench rock slopes on-site. It also allows for potential adjustments to
the slope angle as the rock mass conditions evolve during slope exca-
vation. This approach represents a convenient and effective method for
evaluating slope stability.
6. Conclusion
The seismic response of benched bedding rock slopes was investi-
gated in this study through shaking table tests and complimentary nu-
merical simulations. Four types of slopes with diverse bench widths
were examined, utilizing real-time test data to analyze the distinct
damage phenomena under seismic excitation, acceleration distribution
laws, and earth pressure responses across different platform positions.
Moreover, a dynamic stability equation Sqt was proposed as an index for
assessing slope stability. The key findings are summarized as follows:
(1) The test outcomes show that the AACs at the internal corners of
benches are lower compared to those at the external corners. This
phenomenon is attributed to the considerable stress concentra-
tion induced by the variations of slope gradients, where the in-
ternal corner of the bench serves as the stress concentration zone.
Additionally, the external surfaces of the slope, being less con-
strained, are more susceptible to seismic excitations. Hence,
mitigating steep changes in slope topography is a primary focus
in actual slope projects. Implementing rounded internal corners
helps alleviate stress concentration and reduces the likelihood of
potential damage.
(2) The benched bedding rock slopes experience shattering damage
due to the combined influence of dynamic earth pressure and
residual earth pressure. While dynamic earth pressure dissipates
after the excitation ends, residual earth pressure persists within
the slope, leading to further deformation until noticeable cracks
emerge. Consequently, the progressive growth of earthquake-
induced residual earth pressure should not be overlooked, and
real-time monitoring of earth pressure response is vital for slope
engineering, especially at the internal corners.
(3) The stability of the slope increases with the widening of the slope
platform, as evidenced by the reduction in failure phenomena
compared to slopes with uniform bench widths of 2 m, 4 m, and 6
m. However, the slope with uneven bench width exhibits more
severe failure phenomena. Therefore, slopes with uniform bench
widths generally demonstrate better seismic stability than those
with uneven bench widths.
(4) The numerical simulation results show that slopes with wider
platforms at lower elevations (e.g., the first bench) exhibit a
higher density of shear plastic zones compared to those with
platforms at higher elevations (e.g., the third bench), making the
slope more prone to sliding along the structural planes. Addi-
tionally, when the wider platforms are located at lower elevations
of the slope, the slope is more likely to experience bench-scale
failure.
(5) The Sqt effectively measures the dynamic stability of the slope.
The calculated results are consistent with the damage law
observed in shaking table model tests and align well with nu-
merical simulations, demonstrating the high applicability of the
equation for benched bedding rock slopes with varying geological
structures and landform patterns.
