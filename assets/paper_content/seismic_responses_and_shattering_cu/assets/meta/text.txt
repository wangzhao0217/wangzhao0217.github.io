Bedding parallel stepped rock slopes exist widely in nature and are used in slope engineering. They are
characterized by complex topography and geological structure and are vulnerable to shattering under
strong earthquakes. However, no previous studies have assessed the mechanisms underlying seismic
failure in rock slopes. In this study, large-scale shaking table tests and numerical simulations were
conducted to delineate the seismic failure mechanism in terms of acceleration, displacement, and earth
pressure responses combined with shattering failure phenomena. The results reveal that acceleration
response mutations usually occur within weak interlayers owing to their inferior performance, and these
mutations may transform into potential sliding surfaces, thereby intensifying the nonlinear seismic
response characteristics. Cumulative permanent displacements at the internal corners of the berms can
induce quasi-rigid displacements at the external corners, leading to greater permanent displacements at
the internal corners. Therefore, the internal corners are identiﬁed as the most susceptible parts of the
slope. In addition, the concept of baseline offset was utilized to explain the mechanism of earth pressure
responses, and the result indicates that residual earth pressures at the internal corners play a dominant
role in causing deformation or shattering damage. Four evolutionary deformation phases characterize
the processes of seismic responses and shattering failure of the bedding parallel stepped rock slope, i.e.
the formation of tensile cracks at the internal corners of the berm, expansion of tensile cracks and
bedding surface dislocation, development of vertical tensile cracks at the rear edge, and rock mass
slipping leading to slope instability. Overall, this study provides a scientiﬁc basis for the seismic design of
engineering slopes and offers valuable insights for further studies on preventing seismic disasters in
bedding parallel stepped rock slopes.

1. Introduction
Owing to the regional dynamics associated with the complex
geology, the high-intensity seismic zone of southwest China
frequently experiences a fundamental phenomenon known as su-
perﬁcial cracking of rock masses at the Earth's surface. This
phenomenon creates a geologically vulnerable environment for the
slopes in the region. Generally, shattered slopes are the product of
earthquakes. The structural planes in the rock mass are damaged
and developed due to strong earthquakes; however, there is no
obvious macroscopic deformation (He et al., 2020). Under multi-
stage earthquakes, these cracks further extend and expand, causing
additional damage to the integrity of the rock mass structures on
slopes (Ning et al., 2019; Song et al., 2019). For example, after the
2008 Ms 8.0 Wenchuan earthquake, severely affected areas expe-
rienced widespread shattering of deformable rock masses on the
slope surfaces and within the slopes (Dong et al., 2020). Shattered
deformable slopes in the high-intensity seismic zone of southwest
China are characterized by numerous seismically induced cracks

cracks exhibit a distinctive morphology, extending along different
slope aspects and varying degrees of expansion over signiﬁcant
distances. Generally, seismically induced shattering cracks propa-
gate through the internal and external corners on the slope surfaces
or the contact surfaces between different lithologies on slopes.
They can also propagate inside the rock mass. During aftershocks,
the slope deforms continuously in the gravitational ﬁeld because of
the existing shattering cracks until it reaches the instability or
failure stage, signiﬁcantly affecting the post-seismic stability of
engineering slopes (Xiao et al., 2010; Song et al., 2021a). During the
Wenchuan earthquake, the Chediguan slope shattered and dis-
integrated and generated a massive rock of 5  104 kg, which
collided with several bridge piers. This collision led to the cata-
strophic collapse of the bridge deck by more than 100 m, causing
six casualties. A similar catastrophic event occurred in Sanxi village
near Dujiangyan City during the Wenchuan earthquake. The
seismic activity caused instability and triggered the failure of the
shattering slope, causing 48 casualties, with 11 individuals missing.
Investigations of geological disasters reveal that there are 588
seismically induced unstable shattering slopes in Qingchuan
County after the Wenchuan earthquake, accounting for almost half
of the total geological hazards (Xin et al., 2019; Pu et al., 2020).
The behavior of rocks under dynamic loadings signiﬁcantly
differs from static loadings due to rate-dependent mechanical
properties, resulting in distinct cracking modes at different loading
rates. The dynamic response of brittle materials, including rocks, is
inﬂuenced by several factors. These factors include the loading rate
or strain rate, the duration of the loading event, the conﬁning
pressure, and the inherent properties of the material (Meyers,1994;
Wang et al., 2013). One key dynamic effect is wave propagation,
where elastic waves transmit energy through the material. Lak et al.
(2019) derived a general Green's function solution for elastic wave
propagation and determined the associated strain and stress ﬁelds
using the theory of elasticity. Understanding the transition of
cracking modes relies on the introduction to the concept of a rate-
dependent strength ﬁeld. To examine the impact of structural
planes on slope dynamics, Xu et al. (2023) employed the combined
ﬁnite-discrete element method (FDEM). Their study demonstrated
that the enhanced FDEM approach accurately reproduced dynamic
evolution processes and failure patterns in jointed rock slopes
subjected to seismic loads. Moreover, it provided a quantitative
assessment of the dynamic stability of such slopes. Theoretically,
slope shattering represents a type of cracking without sliding,
caused by strong earthquakes, and it is a widespread potential
geological hazard. The slope shattering has generally been over-
looked during the evaluation of geological disasters or seismic
precaution intensity research owing to its inherent characteristics.
It is crucial to investigate the stability of seismically induced slope
shattering as it can contribute to the development of improved
prediction methods and prevention strategies for post-earthquake
slope hazards, mitigating the risk effects (Shinoda et al., 2015; Feng
et al., 2021). Normally, the seismically induced shattering of slopes
with different lithologic characteristics generates different seismic
responses and failure patterns. During the 1999 Chi-Chi earth-
quake,
the
clustering
of
earthquake-induced
landslides
that
occurred near ridgetops is mainly attributed to the topographic
ampliﬁcation of ground shaking. The intense ground shaking led to
shattering and landslide erosions, particularly in areas with
elevated topography. This phenomenon resulted in further insta-
bility and led to the erosion of ridgetops. In contrast, the shattering
phenomenon randomly appears on slope surfaces beside sloping
ridges after an earthquake (Che et al., 2016). An analysis of seismic
energy transmission ratios in the weak zones of the slope reveals
that shattering failure is generally developed ﬁrst at the slope crest
and slope toe of the sliding mass owing to resonance effects (Fan
et al., 2017). The shattering of the rock mass induced by ground
shaking reduces the frictional strength of the substrate, thereby
causing shear failure of the toe of a large-scale landslide (Huang
et al., 2012). Furthermore, earthquakes induce additional stress
within the slopes, particularly when the stress is horizontally ori-
ented and perpendicular to the normal direction of the slope. This
increased stress often results in an excessive occurrence of land-
slides. The geological structure of a slope is prone to destruction
under seismic excitation and results in the formation of numerous
joints and ﬁssures. These joints and ﬁssures act as preferential
pathways for surface water inﬁltration, further exacerbating the
slope instability (Peng et al., 2019; Dong et al., 2020). Earthquake-
induced landslides tend to be concentrated in speciﬁc zones such
as reliefs, slopes, and topographic roughness (Yin et al., 2010). The
post-earthquake shattering of slopes is widely acknowledged as
one of the primary causes of collapse and landslides. Ten years after
the Wenchuan earthquake, shattered and induced failure was
observed on hillslopes located 250 km NE of the mainshock
epicenter (Fan et al., 2019). A study by Del Gaudio et al. (2018)
suggested that the resonance phenomenon of the slopes is
strongly inﬂuenced by shattered Precambrian limestone. The red-
bed landslide exhibits a seismic failure mechanism characterized
by tension, crushing, shattering, and sliding. Furthermore, owing to
the steep slope gradient, presence of well-developed tensile frac-
tures, and frequent occurrence of aftershocks and rainstorms,
toppling and small collapses can occur at the back scarp of the
remaining slopes (Li et al., 2019). Furthermore, originally intact
slopes may eventually develop into shattered slopes, facilitating
postseismic
rockfalls.
Additionally,
earthquake-induced
local
landslides often initiate the failure of shattered rock masses or
regoliths near the ridge of a high and steep slope, resulting in
destruction owing to the large momentum and entrainment po-
tential of the moving mass. Slope ridges are particularly vulnerable
to the topographic ampliﬁcation of seismic shaking, which can lead
them to aggravate weathering and rain-triggered failure years or
decades after the mainshock (Wu et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2022).
Previous studies on seismically induced shattering slopes have
primarily focused on analyzing dynamic responses and failure
patterns. However, the dynamic response mechanisms and cumu-
lative shattering effects of bedding parallel stepped rock slopes
remain unclear. Two scientiﬁc issues need to be addressed: (1)
propagation mechanisms of seismic motion in complex rock
masses of the slope, and (2) seismically induced evolution mech-
anisms of shattering slopes (Xin et al., 2020; Qi et al., 2022).
Resolving these issues will be beneﬁcial for evaluating and pre-
dicting slope shattering. Thus, we conducted a series of shaking
table model tests and numerical simulations, as they can capture
the entire process of slope shattering, not only on slope surfaces but
also on slope bodies with different berm patterns (Xin et al., 2018;
Su et al., 2021). The study area and the preparations for the shaking
table model tests are described in Sections 2 and 3, respectively.
The experimental ﬁndings focus on bedding parallel stepped rock
slopes that consist of weak interlayers and are subjected to
increasing intensities of Wolong seismic motions. This study
compare the failure patterns and topographic changes in slope
models with varying berm widths, based on the measured accel-
eration, displacement, and earth pressure data, during seismic ex-
citations. Finally, based on the results, the seismic response
mechanisms
and
cumulative
effects
of
slope

3. Materials and methods
3.1. Laboratory equipment
The system consists of a 4 m  6 m (length  width) platform
operated by eight hydraulic actuators that can produce excitations
in three directions with six degrees of freedom (Fig. 2). The system
is equipped with a response signal collection and analysis system,
which is controlled by a servo controller via a controlling software
installed on a computer. The actuators positioned in the horizontal
and vertical directions have different dynamic capacities, allowing
the platform to attain a maximum horizontal acceleration of 15 m/
s2 and a maximum vertical acceleration of 10 m/s2, corresponding
to maximum strokes of 300 mm and 150 mm, respectively. Addi-
tionally, the maximum payload of this shaking table system is
4  104 kg, and an operational frequency range of 0e60 Hz.
Therefore, either a single signal with a certain amplitude and fre-
quency (i.e. sine wave) or a ﬁeld seismic wave with complex fre-
quency components can be selected as the input waveforms to
investigate the dynamic response of the research objects.
A large-scale rigid model container was fabricated to contain the
model slopes. It has dimensions of 3.6 m  1.5 m  2.1 m
(length  width  height) and is rigidly ﬁxed on the shaking table
by high shearing strength bolts for the tests. To minimize the
reﬂection and refraction effects of the input seismic wave on the
boundaries of the model container, two polystyrene foams with a
thickness of 20 cm were steeply lined at the gaps between the
model container ends and the model slopes. The foam board also
serves to absorb the energy of the reﬂected wave from the side wall
of the rigid model container and simulate the dissipation effect of
the seismic wave across the inﬁnite ﬁeld. The elastic modulus,
thickness, Poisson's ratio and density of ﬂexible materials are
carefully considered. To further enhance the accuracy of the tests,
the following measures were taken to mitigate the effects of friction
between the model container and model slopes: plexiglass boards
were carefully cleaned and coated with Vaseline before being ﬁxed
to the model container. Then, a transparent friction-reduce ﬁlm was
pasted onto the inner sides of the model container and Vaseline
was also coated on the surface of the ﬁlm. By means of these
measures, the effects of friction between the model container and
the model slopes could be effectively mitigated.
3.2. Similitude relations and parameters
The shaking table model test should utilize similitude relations
to replicate the dynamic response phenomena of the prototype in
terms of geometric dimensions, time, mass, boundary conditions,
and initial conditions. The acceleration was rationally selected as a
controlling parameter because it had an evident impact on the
dynamic response of the model slope in the shaking table test. The
similarity ratio of the acceleration was determined to be one for the
shaking model test because it differs from the centrifuge model
test, which can change the acceleration, considerably (Sun et al.,
2020; Zhu et al., 2021). The geometric size of the model slope
with a scaling factor of 25 was also selected as a controlling
parameter, which was limited by the geometric size of the shaking
table and model container. According to the Buckingham p theo-
rem and dimensional analysis, geometric dimension (L), density (r),
and acceleration (a) were chosen as the three fundamental con-
trolling parameters. This section allows for the derivation of the
similitude relation of other physical variables from the three
fundamental controlling parameters. In this study, a total of 13
parameters were considered, and these parameters satisfy the

4. Analysis of the test results
4.1. The seismic responses of accelerations
The inclinations of weak interlayers and the patterns of the
slope surface play a crucial role in determining the propagation and
reﬂection paths of seismic waves within the slope. This, in turn,
inﬂuences the response laws of accelerations in slope bodies and on
slope surfaces. Understanding the variation laws of the acceleration
ampliﬁcation coefﬁcient (AAC) can provide valuable insights into
the seismic response characteristics at different positions in the
slope interiors and on the surfaces. Furthermore, it can reveal how
weak interlayers and slope-surface patterns affect the seismic re-
sponses of the entire slope body. In this study, the AAC was deﬁned
as the absolute value ratio of the peak value difference (i.e. the
difference between positive and negative values on the time history
curve) at each monitoring point to that of the monitoring point at
the bedrock of the slope model.
The AAC gradually increased from the bottom to the top of the
slope and reached the maximum value at the top of the slope
(Fig. 7). This pattern characterizes the elevation ampliﬁcation ef-
fects (EAEs) of the seismically induced acceleration responses in
bedding parallel rock slopes. Seismic waves were reﬂected and
diffracted in the slope interior with increasing elevation and
generated the EAE of the acceleration responses both in the slope
interior and on the slope surface. The AAC exhibited a nonlinear
increasing trend from the slope interior to the shallow surfaces of
the slope, reaching its maximum values at the slope surfaces. This
represents the surface ampliﬁcation effects (SAEs) of the seismi-
cally induced acceleration responses in bedding rock slopes.
Consequently, seismic waves are reﬂected and transmitted chaot-
ically at slope surfaces with a free-face approximation of slopes,
thereby producing the SAE of acceleration responses. In addition,
there is no constraint but a particular topography on the slope
surface compared to the slope interior, thereby making the slope
surfaces more sensitive to seismic excitations and hence causing
loosening and collapse. In the severe disaster areas of the 2008
Wenchuan earthquake and the 2013 Lushan earthquake, there
were numerous seismic hazards around the slope surfaces of
bedding rock slopes containing weak interlayers than within the
slope interiors. Among the monitoring positions at slope surfaces,
owing to the combined actions of the EAE and SAE, seismic waves
experienced complex reﬂection and superposition at the intersec-
tion between the slope surface and the slope top, giving the
intersection the highest freedom, leading to the conspicuous
ampliﬁcation effect of the seismic waves. Similar AACs were
observed at the external corners of the berms on slope surfaces.
Therefore, the maximum AACs were at the intersections of the
slope surfaces and slope tops, regardless of the berm width, fol-
lowed by the external corners at the third berms, second berms,
and ﬁrst berms (Fig. 7a and b).
The AACs at the internal corners of the berms were lower than
those at the external corner, even though the AACs at the internal
corners increased with the increasing input peak accelerations. This
phenomenon can be attributed to the variations in slope gradients,
which induce a conspicuous stress concentration, that is, the
topography of the slope surface can directly change the seismic
responses of the AACs (Fig. 7c and d). In addition, the seismic re-
sponses of the AACs at the rear edges also reﬂected the EAE and SAE
compared with the AACs at the internal corners of the slopes. It is
worth noting that AACs exhibited nonlinear seismic responses
when the input peak acceleration exceeded 0.6g, especially at the
rear edges of the slopes (Fig. 7e and f). During seismic excitation,
mutations in the acceleration response usually occur at weak in-
terlayers owing to their weaker physical and mechanical proper-
ties. This transforms the weak interlayers into potential sliding
surfaces, thereby intensifying the nonlinear seismic response
characteristics of the entire slope.
The differences in AACs between the external corners of the
slope berm and the internal corners, as well as the differences
between the internal corners of the slope berm and the rear edge,
were calculated. The results indicated that the AACs at the external
corners of the slope berm were consistently larger than those at the

4.2. The seismic responses of displacements at slope surfaces
Analyzing the seismically induced permanent displacements in
the slope interior and on the slope surface is crucial for under-
standing the seismic response mechanisms and the macro-
shattering phenomena that occur throughout the entire slope.
The cumulative permanent displacements were plotted against
input peak accelerations based on the displacement response laws
of the bedding parallel stepped rock slopes (Fig. 8). These diagrams
in Fig. 8 provide a visual representation of the progressive accu-
mulation of permanent displacements caused by multiple seismic
excitations.
Generally, the amplitudes of permanent displacements of slopes
increase signiﬁcantly with increasing seismic excitations and rela-
tive elevations, which causes the maximum permanent displace-
ment to occur at the slope top, and the minimum permanent
displacement to occur at the slope toe. However, in this series of

combined effects of seismic excitations and weak interlayers. In a
bedding parallel stepped rock slope that contains multiple weak
interlayers, the dynamic response is inﬂuenced not only by the
topography of the stepped slope surface but also by the properties
of the weak interlayers. The deteriorating physical and mechanical
parameters of different rock masses can determine the position of
the ﬁrst seismically induced shattering crack. Different berm
widths lead to complex reﬂections and diffractions of seismic
waves with high energies, which causes cracks in tension at the
internal corners of the berms, leading to shattering damage or even
collapse of the entire slope.
Generally, the permanent displacements of bedding rock slope
with identical berm widths linearly increase and cumulate with
increasing input peak accelerations, especially when excited by
seismic motions ranging from 0.2g to 0.6g (Fig. 8a and b). When the
input peak acceleration was greater than 0.6g, the apparent per-
manent displacements increased at the internal corners of the
berms on the slope surface. Especially, the increase in cumulative
permanent displacement was greater than 160% at the rear edge of
the slope top, and it exceeded 180% at the external corner of the
slope top. Seismic excitations with high energies push the slopes
away from the absorbing boundaries, generating tensile cracks at
the rear edge of the slope and increasing its degrees of freedom.
This process invariably incurs seismic failure to the slope, although
it does not cause collapse at this stage. As shown in Fig. 8c and d,
the cumulative permanent displacements increased on the bedding
rock slope surface with different berm widths when the input peak
acceleration was greater than 0.4g. To analyze the growth trend of
the permanent displacement for slope with different berm widths,
the ratios of the permanent displacement for input peak accelera-
tions of 0.4g, 0.6g, 0.8g, and 1g to the permanent displacement for
an input peak acceleration of 0.2g were calculated. The results
indicate that the increase in permanent displacement of the slope
was slow when the input peak acceleration increased from 0.2g to
0.4g. However, when the input peak acceleration further increased
from 0.4g to 1g, the permanent displacement of the slope increased
rapidly (Fig. 8e and f). This indicates that the seismic stability of
bedding rock slope with different berm widths is more vulnerable
than that with identical berm widths. When the input peak accel-
eration reached 1g, the cracks in the slope continuously expanded,
and the cracks in the ﬁrst berm deepened and widened. Ultimately,
the ﬁrst berm was peeled off by strong seismic inertial forces at a
distance of 20 cm, resulting in larger displacements in the ﬁrst and
second berms than those at the slope top. However, because the
cumulative permanent displacements at the internal corners of the
berms can push the external corners into quasi-rigid displace-
ments, it is common for the cumulative permanent displacements
at the internal corners to be larger than those at the external cor-
ners regardless of the berm widths of the bedding rock slopes. Thus,
the internal corners are the most vulnerable parts of the entire
slope. For bedding rock slope with identical berm widths, the dy-
namic response of the rock mass above the third berm is stronger,
making it the key area of the seismic instability failure of the slope.
However, for bedding rock slope with different berm widths, the
seismic inertial forces are stronger on the second berm because of
the wider berm and larger rock mass. This force converges to
become a resultant force in the forward and downward directions,
leading to shattering cracks or even the collapse of the entire slope.
Therefore, the top of the slope and wider berm are the seismic
fortiﬁcation zones for the two slope types with different slope
surface patterns.
4.3. Seismic responses of earth pressures
Seismically induced variations in earth pressures, along with the
corresponding post-seismic earth pressure distributions in slopes,
play a crucial role in determining the dynamic deformation
mechanisms and seismic damage modes. These factors are vital for
evaluating the post-earthquake stability of engineering slopes.
During the seismic excitation, signiﬁcant deformation occurred in
both the rock mass and weak interlayers of the slope, particularly
under high-amplitude input accelerations. These deformation
generated residual earth pressure, which was measured using earth
pressure cells. The recorded data exhibit a baseline drift, as shown
in Fig. 9, indicating a gradual and persistent change in the earth
pressure over time. The baseline drift value in the time-history
diagram of the earth pressure response can characterize the post-

The dynamic earth pressure dissipates after seismic excitation,

4.4. The damage phenomena of shattering slope
As shown in Fig.10, the seismic shattering phenomena observed
in this series of shaking table model tests can precisely replicate the
post-earthquake shattering phenomena in practical slope engi-
neering. The seismic motion with high energies gradually pushed
the slope with different berm widths away from the absorbing
boundary, yielding an approximately 8 cm tensile crack and
destroying the integrity and stability of the entire slope (Fig. 10a).
The rock mass in front of this tensile crack successively squeezed
the lower berms during seismic excitation, which was combined
with the seismic inertial forces of the wider berm, creating tensile
cracks at the internal corner of the wider berm. Subsequently, the
wider berm tended to move forward and downward, allowing the
ﬁrst berm to spall from the lower part of the slope (Fig. 10b).
Generally, seismic excitation with high energy can increase the
stress concentration at the internal corner of the berm, generating
tensile cracks perpendicular to the excitation direction, which then
propagate to numerous secondary cracks within the rock mass and
weak interlayers (Fig. 10c). These seismic shattering phenomena
are often observed in practical bedding rock slope engineering with
ﬂuctuating slope surfaces, such as the post-earthquake Dongjia-
liang landslide in Qingchuan County, China. The shattering cracks
propagated following the slope surface pattern over a long distance
and were accompanied by many secondary cracks (Fig. 10d). The
combined effects of the platform moving forward above the
external corner and the slope deformation below the external
corner increased the stress concentration at the external corner of
the second berm, which induced tensile-shearing cracks. Moreover,
the ﬁrst berm constrained the slope deformation below the
external corner of the second berm, and the slope surface was
peeled off by the strong seismic inertial forces (Fig. 10e). These
shattering cracks appearing on slope surfaces and in external cor-
ners have been widely reported by post-earthquake surveys for
bedding rock slope engineering (e.g. the similar phenomena to the
Feishaguan landslide in Wenchuan County (Fig. 10f). Seismic shat-
tering cracks initiate at the bulge positions of the slope surface,
subsequently leading to signiﬁcant collapse. It is important to
highlight the cyclic tensile-shearing effects induced by seismic
activity on the entire slope. These effects result in deformation and,

5. Numerical simulation
5.1. Numerical modeling
Flac3D was employed to study the dynamic response mecha-
nisms and the cumulative shattering effects of the full-scale
bedding parallel stepped rock slope. To simulate seismic wave
propagation in a continuous medium, the maximum mesh size
should be less than or equal to 1/8 to 1/10 of the minimum length of
the seismic wave. Therefore, in this study, the maximum mesh size
of the elements was set to 0.8 m. Ten slope models, cases 1e10,
were designed to investigate the effects of different sliding surface
locations, sliding surface inclination angles (SSIA), and berm widths
on the seismic response of the slopes. For different sliding surface
locations, the sliding surface was designed to expose the internal
and external corners of the second berm. Models having different
sliding surfaces with inclination angles of 5, 35, and 60 and berm
widths of 2 m, 4 m, and 6 m were designed. Additionally, a single-
surface slope scheme was designed to compare the changes in
slope stability after excavation into the berms. The numerical
calculation model and dynamic boundary settings are shown in
Fig. 12. A free-ﬁeld boundary was established around the model to
simulate an inﬁnite ﬁeld and minimize the reﬂections and energy
radiation at the model boundary, and there were Rayleigh dampers
between the slope model and the free-ﬁeld boundary. In addition,
local dampers were installed to reduce the computation time. The
Rayleigh damping with a minimum critical damping ratio of
xmin ¼ 5%, a minimum central frequency of umin ¼ 5 Hz, and a local
damping value of 0.157 (equivalent to 5% of critical damping) was
applied to the numerical simulation model (Itasca, 2014; Lin et al.,
2017; Ling et al., 2023). To compare with the results of the shaking
table model tests, the same monitoring points were employed in
both the numerical simulation models and physical slope models,
which can monitor and record real-time data.

5.3. Expansion analysis of factors
From the distribution of the plastic zone, it can be observed that
as the berm width increases, the area of the plastic zone gradually
decreases and is primarily distributed at the slope crest and inter-
nal corner of the berm (Fig. 15aec). Therefore, as the berm width
increases, the slope stability is enhanced. Under seismic excitation,
the slope exhibits the characteristic of an EAE. The horizontal
displacement and inertial forces generated at the slope crest are
more likely to cause the expansion and extension of cracks, leading
to shattering damages to the stepped slope. The internal corner of
the berm is a weak as well as a stress concentration zone of the
slope. Under seismic excitation, the stress at the internal corner of
the berm exceeds the shear strength of the slope, resulting in the
initial failure of the stepped slope occurring at the internal corner of
the berm. The failure process of the stepped slope in the shaking
table test series demonstrates that the internal corner of the berm
is a weak zone and is susceptible to cracking and failure under
seismic excitation. For the single-surface slope, the distribution of
the plastic zone is larger than that of the stepped slope, indicating
that the stability of the single-surface slope is poorer under seismic
excitation (Fig. 15d). The plastic zone almost extends through the
weak interlayer, which indicates that the rock material near the
structural plane has exceeded its yield strength, exhibiting an
overall failure phenomenon. Thus, stepped landforms and their
geological settings can signiﬁcantly control the local seismic
response. This method is an environmentally friendly artiﬁcial
reinforcement technique that can transform the internal failure of a
slope into a superﬁcial failure.
The numerical simulation results show that when the SSIA is
35, the slope stability is the worst, and plastic failure occurs at the
crest and internal corner of the berm (Fig.15f). At an SSIA of 35, the
SSIA and the slope angle are nearly identical, indicating that there is
only a small angle between the sliding surface and the overall slope
surface, which reduces the resistance of the slope to sliding. The
smaller the angle, the greater the sliding force component along the
slope surface, thereby increasing the probability of slope sliding.
Under seismic excitations, the shear stress between the sliding
surface and the overall slope surface becomes the highest, pro-
moting the sliding of the rock mass. Conversely, when the SSIA is 5,
the slope is less prone to failure, as shown in Fig. 15e. At a small
SSIA, the component of the sliding force is nearly perpendicular to
the slope surface, resulting in a smaller sliding force acting on the
slope. Therefore, the slope stability is better in this case. When the
SSIA is 60, the SSIA is signiﬁcantly larger than the slope angle, and
the slope is less likely to experience sliding failure (Fig. 15g). A
greater SSIA leads to a larger sliding force component perpendic-
ular to the slope surface, enhancing the resistance of the slope to
sliding and improving its stability.
When the sliding surface is exposed at the internal corner of the
second berm, it leads to a more pronounced seismic response
because of the stress concentration in that area (Fig. 15h). This
causes a larger portion of the rock mass to exceed its yield strength,
resulting in irreversible deformation. The stability of the slope is
inﬂuenced by the position of the sliding surface. When the sliding
surface is located at the internal corner of the second berm, the
internal corner is the ﬁrst to crack when subjected to seismic
excitation. As the peak ground motion increases, the cracks pro-
gressively expand, creating favorable conditions for slope sliding.
The cracking of the internal corner triggers stress redistribution and
shear sliding along the sliding surface, ultimately resulting in
shattering failure and slope instability. In contrast, when the sliding
surface is exposed at the external corner of the second berm, the
seismic damage is milder. An analysis of the plastic zone distribu-
tion reveals that when the sliding surface is exposed at the second
berm, the plastic zone primarily occurs above the second berm and
at the rear edge of the slope, covering a smaller area (Fig.15i). When
the inclination angle of the sliding surface is modiﬁed, it can be
exposed at the external corner of the second berm. By signiﬁcantly
increasing the SSIA compared with the slope angle, the shear stress
along the sliding surface becomes relatively small and insufﬁcient

6. Discussion
According to the principle of controlling variables, two different
slope terrains of bedding parallel stepped rock slope were selected
to study the seismic response mechanisms and shattering cumu-
lative effects using shaking table tests and numerical simulations.
In the shaking table tests, the acceleration, displacement, and earth
pressure distribution patterns were analyzed to elucidate the
shattering failure mechanisms of the bedding parallel stepped rock
slope. By observing the progressive failure process of the slope, the
shattering failure mechanisms were explained. The shaking table
tests provided valuable insights into the response patterns of the
slope under seismic excitations. In the numerical simulations, the
displacement and earth pressure responses were analyzed to vali-
date the displacement and failure modes of the slope. Furthermore,
an expansion analysis was performed to investigate the effects of
different variables such as sliding surface locations, sliding surface
inclination angles, and berm widths. This analysis aimed to un-
derstand how these variables inﬂuenced the behavior of the slope,
including its stability and failure mechanisms. By combining the
results from the shaking table tests and numerical simulations, a
comprehensive understanding of the seismic response mecha-
nisms and shattering cumulative effects of the bedding parallel
stepped rock slope was obtained. This research contributes to the
knowledge of slope engineering and provides insights for designing
and mitigating the seismic risks associated with such slope terrains.
The results of the shaking table tests reveal that the slope ex-
hibits an acceleration ampliﬁcation effect. As the input seismic
acceleration gradually increases, the slope experiences a nonlinear
growth in acceleration ampliﬁcation. The stepped shape of the
slope surface contributes to this behavior. The external corners of
the berm have relatively less constraint compared to the internal
corners, allowing for greater freedom of movement. Consequently,
these external corners are more prone to signiﬁcant dynamic
displacement responses under seismic excitation. Conversely, the
internal corners of the berm experience greater constraints,
resulting in larger earth pressure responses. As the accumulated
residual earth pressure reaches a critical threshold surpassing the
shear strength of the rock mass, cracks begin to form in the slope.
With further increases in the peak input acceleration, these cracks
propagate and new ones emerge. This leads to the shattering failure
of the slope, triggering a nonlinear dynamic response.
The progressive failure process and failure modes of the bedding
parallel stepped rock slope are closely related to the geometric
shape of the slope, the mechanical properties of the rock mass, and
the seismic dynamic characteristics. The study of the progressive
failure process of the slope under seismic action can provide
valuable guidance for engineering practice. By delving into the
progressive failure process, weaknesses and potential hazardous
areas of the slope can be identiﬁed and evaluated, thus providing
accurate recommendations and strategies for the design, con-
struction, and maintenance of the slope. For this series of experi-
ments, the progressive failure process of the slope can be divided
into four stages: stage 1, the formation of tensile cracks at the in-
ternal corners of the berm, stage 2, the expansion of tensile cracks
and dislocation of the bedding surface, stage 3, the development of
vertical tensile cracks at the rear edge, and stage 4, the slipping of
the rock mass and loss of slope stability (Fig. 17a). In the case of the
slope with different berm widths, when the peak input acceleration
reaches 0.6g, tensile cracks ﬁrst appear at the internal corners of the
second berm due to its larger width. With the continued increase in
peak input acceleration, these tensile cracks extend downward and
reach near the sliding surface. At this point, the slope is divided into
blocks due to the downward propagation of the cracks. The upper

Under repeated seismic activity, the stress state of the rock mass

types of slope failure. At the internal corners of the berm, once the
tensile stress induced by seismic forces exceeds the tensile strength
of the rock mass, it directly results in tensile failure of the rock
mass, forming tensile cracks or failure planes. Near the sliding
surface of the slope, the shear stress caused by seismic forces may
exceed the shear strength of the rock mass, leading to sliding failure
of the rock layers on the sliding surface. This means that the
structural planes between the rock layers undergo shear and form
distinct sliding surfaces. Regarding the rear edge of the slope, under
the inﬂuence of strong seismic inertial forces, both tensile and
shear stresses can occur simultaneously. The rock mass at the rear

forming steep tensile cracks at the rear edge. This phenomenon is

For future research, it is scientiﬁcally signiﬁcant to analyze the
seismic response mechanisms and focus on the shattering cumu-
lative effects. This exploration will involve establishing the rela-
tionship between physical mechanical parameters and shattering
evolutionary processes by changing the geological structures of
slopes, the terrain morphology of slopes and the dynamic me-
chanical parameter performances.

response mechanisms and the shattering cumulative effects in
bedding parallel stepped rock slopes through large-scale shaking
table tests. The research involved comprehensive analysis of test
data, including measurements of seismic responses such as accel-

model slopes was thoroughly examined. To validate the shaking
table test results, numerical simulations were conducted, providing
insights into dynamic displacement responses and the distribution
of plastic zones within the slopes. Based on the comprehensive
analysis of the test results and numerical simulations, the following
key ﬁndings are summarized:
(1) The internal corners of the berms exhibited lower AACs
compared to the external corners, even though the AACs at
the internal corners increased with increasing input peak
accelerations. This phenomenon can be attributed to varia-
tions in slope gradients, which create distinct stress con-
centrations. The topography of the slope surface directly
inﬂuences the seismic responses of the AACs.
(2) Multiple stages of seismic excitation led to the generation of
extensive cracks, fracturing the rock mass and increasing the
wave ﬁltering effects within the slope body. The attenuations
of the AACs in bedding rock slopes with identical berm
widths were lower than those in slopes with different berm
widths, due to differences in damage development charac-
teristics and cumulative velocities.
(3) Cumulative permanent displacements at the internal corners
of the berms induced quasi-rigid displacements at the
external corners, resulting in larger cumulative permanent
displacements at the internal corners regardless of the berm
widths of the bedding rock slopes. Thus, the internal corners
were identiﬁed as the most vulnerable parts of the entire
slope.
(4) Initial cracks facilitated seismic energy dissipation and hin-
dered the accumulation of residual earth pressure, poten-
tially leading to nonlinear responses in earth pressure.
Seismic energies were transformed into deformable poten-
tial energies within the slope interior before seismic shat-
tering occurred. Consequently, the initial residual earth
pressure at the internal corner of the second berm was
higher than that at the internal corner of the ﬁrst berm,
regardless of the berm width.
(5) Seismic excitation with high energy intensiﬁed stress con-
centration at the internal corner of the berm, resulting in
tensile cracks perpendicular to the excitation direction.
These cracks propagated to numerous secondary cracks
within the rock mass and weak interlayers, exhibiting
seismic shattering phenomena frequently observed in prac-
tical bedding rock slope engineering with ﬂuctuating slope
surfaces, such as the post-earthquake Dongjialiang landslide
in Qingchuan County, China.

Chunlei Xin has been engaged in teaching and research in
the ﬁeld of seismic interaction in geotechnical engineering
and underground structural engineering. He obtained his
Master degree and PhD in Bridge and Tunnel Engineering
from Southwest Jiaotong University in 2011 and 2016,
respectively. He pursued joint PhD studies at Rensselaer
Polytechnic Institute from 2013 to 2014. Since 2022, he
has been working as a researcher at the State Key Labo-
ratory of Geological Hazard Prevention and Geological
Environment Protection. He was recognized as a high-
level talent in overseas studies from Sichuan Province
and the transportation industry. He was also regarded as
a key teacher at Chengdu University of Technology.
Throughout his career, he has successfully led and partic-
ipated in over 10 research projects funded by the National
Natural Science Foundation of China and provincial-level
research programs. He has published more than 20 SCI-
indexed papers as the ﬁrst author or corresponding
author. His contributions have been acknowledged with
a second-class prize for scientiﬁc and technological prog-
ress at the provincial and ministerial levels.
