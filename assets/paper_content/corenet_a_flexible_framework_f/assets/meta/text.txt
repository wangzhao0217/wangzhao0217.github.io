A B S T R A C T
Mode shift to cycling is a policy objective in many countries. However, cycling infrastructure designs often fail to 
produce the joined-up, direct, and safe networks needed for trips to switch. Planned interventions rarely align 
with demand, creating disjointed networks and ineffective investment. This paper introduces a new ‘corenet’ 
method for developing core cycling networks that provides complete and connected infrastructure designs for 
places with or without existing cycling networks. The method demonstrates its application in the Network 
Planning Tool for Scotland (NPT) to support city-wide network designs and serves as the foundation for the 
Network Planning Workspace (NPW) tool, enabling local authorities to plan cycle networks through a structured, 
tiered approach. The method integrates multiple datasets, including estimates of current and future demand and 
network geometries from multiple sources. It enables users to generate designs suited to local needs by balancing 
network cohesion, coverage, and directness metrics. The ‘corenet’ method also simulates network growth, 
supporting strategic expansion of cycling infrastructure in a prioritised sequence. Furthermore, a set of network 
evaluation metrics has been developed, enabling rigorous assessment of both proposed and existing cycling 
networks. We conclude by discussing how the method can be extended in future research and integrated with 
established processes for strategic network planning.
1. Introduction
Modal shift away from private cars is key to tackling environmental, 
economic, and social challenges in modern cities. While public transport 
and demand response transit (DRT) receive attention, active travel 
modes offer relatively low costs and high potential to reduce external­
ities while improving public health. Though ‘active travel’ often means 
walking and cycling, it includes various human-powered modes like 
wheeling, scooting, and e-bikes (Cook et al., 2022). This paper focuses 
on cycling, which best replaces short car trips due to its speed, range, 
and capacity to carry goods and passengers. Recognising cycling's ben­
efits, many countries, including the UK and its devolved nations, have 
set ambitious targets to increase cycling trips (Wardman et al., 1997; 
Aldred et al., 2017).
Driven by national and regional policies, many local governments 
have set targets and implemented interventions to enable cycling up­
take. Edinburgh's Active Travel Action Plan aims to make cycling a 
“realistic choice for all” (City of Edinburgh Council, 2023). This plan 
forms part of the broader City Mobility Plan, evaluated against key in­
dicators including increasing dedicated cycling space beyond the 215 
km recorded in 2019 (City of Edinburgh Council, 2024). These initia­
tives align with Scottish government objectives to promote sustainable 
transport, with the Cycling Action Plan targeting 10% of everyday 
journeys by bike (Transport Scotland, 2017). Cities worldwide have

In addition to the length of cycling infrastructure, its quality is also 
important, with wide cycle tracks separated from motor traffic more 
likely to get people cycling than other types of infrastructure (Delcl`os- 
Ali´o and den Hoed, 2024). Transport Scotland published design stan­

on guidance from the English Department for Transport (Department for 
Transport, 2020).
With few exceptions (e.g., cities in the Netherlands, Denmark, and 
parts of Germany), low cycling mode shares suggest that investment has 
not been sufficiently large, cost-effective, or both. Despite policy support 
and investment, systemic barriers prevent cycling from becoming the 
main choice for short (1–5 km) urban journeys (Aldred et al., 2017). 
Poor cycling networks remain a critical barrier (Pucher and Buehler, 
2008; Buehler and Dill, 2016b). Practitioners identify several factors 
causing disjointed networks: ‘stop-start’ funding, local opposition, and 
inadequate maintenance (Reynolds, 2025). Limited evidence on effec­
tive network design is also problematic, with many local authorities 
lacking tools and skills to design and evaluate cycling networks.
To address these challenges, transport authorities are developing 
more structured approaches to network planning. Transport Scotland's 
CbD guidance, for instance, emphasises a user-centred approach to 
cycling network development that meets diverse needs, especially for 
new and less confident cyclists. These guidelines seek to overcome the 
limitations of piecemeal infrastructure by requiring networks to align 
with wider policy objectives, employ data-driven planning methods, 
implement strategic network expansion connecting key destinations, 
engage with local communities, and undergo rigorous design reviews. 
However, translating these requirements into actionable network de­
signs remains difficult without specialised methodological support.
This paper introduces ‘corenet’, a method for developing complete 
and connected cycling networks by integrating cycling potential data 
with existing transport networks and local needs. The method was 
developed for the Network Planning Tool for Scotland (NPT), a planning 
support system and web application for strategic cycle network plan­
ning. The NPT provides street-level cycling potential estimates to 
identify high-demand corridors and inform network design. However, 
these detailed network-level results are difficult to convert into action­
able plans. The application presents planners with overwhelming data, 
making it challenging to create prioritised investment plans from 
thousands of map lines. In response to user feedback, primarily from 
transport planners using the Network Planning Tool for Scotland (NPT) 
who highlighted the need for more directly usable outputs, the corenet 
method provides focused, actionable output: a core cycling network to 
guide investment and planning decisions.
To support our NPT project work, the method is provided as an open- 
source package (see ‘Data and Software development’). Though origi­
nally designed for Scotland, the method works in diverse urban contexts 
across the UK and beyond. The corenet methodology has been imple­
mented as the foundation for the Network Planning Workspace (NPW) 
tool, which enables local authorities to plan cycle networks through a 
structured, tiered approach. This implementation demonstrates how the 
framework translates algorithmic network generation into practical 
planning support, allowing practitioners to develop infrastructure de­
signs that balance connectivity, demand, and local constraints. This 
research has four primary objectives: (1) establish a comprehensive 
methodology for designing core cycling networks, (2) demonstrate its 
practical application in Scottish urban areas, (3) evaluate the effec­
tiveness of proposed and existing networks, and (4) offer actionable 
insights and recommendations.
The paper is structured as follows: Section 2 reviews cycling 
network design literature, highlighting challenges and gaps. Section 3 
describes the input datasets, identifies key barriers to complete network 
generation, and presents the hybrid approach adopted to address data 
challenges. Section 4 details the corenet method, providing a compre­
hensive explanation of the network generation process, parameter se­
lection, and growth strategies. Section 5 presents a structured approach 
to network evaluation, applying metrics for cohesion, connectivity, de­
mand alignment, and efficiency to assess networks generated for Scot­
tish cities. Section 6 examines implications for active travel planning, 
addresses challenges and limitations, and proposes future research 
directions.
2. Literature review
Methods for developing and prioritising cycle network plans can be 
divided into four main themes: data-driven approaches in network 
planning, network growth strategies, network-evaluation tools, and 
economic optimisation.
2.1. Data-driven approaches in network planning
Large datasets have enhanced our understanding of cycling behav­
iour patterns. Origin-destination matrices from ‘call detail records’ 
(CDR) are anonymised mobile network data capturing device connec­
tions to cellular towers, which offer valuable empirical evidence of in­
dividual movement patterns. Unlike census-based OD datasets, which 
are aggregated zone-to-zone and spatially ‘jittered’ to approximate 
point-to-point flows, CDR provide observed, time-stamped traces that 
complement traditional travel survey methods by revealing finer spatio- 
temporal travel dynamics (Alexander et al., 2015). Combined with new 
methods, these OD datasets generate cycling potential estimates (Xu 
et al., 2016) and detailed activity schedules (Ballis and Dimitriou, 2020) 
for network planning. GPS devices, cycle counters, bike hire schemes, 
and social media provide insights into cycling preferences, routes, 
speeds, and popular destinations (Romanillos et al., 2016).
Network planning tools use various datasets to prioritise cycling 
infrastructure investment (Larsen et al., 2013). The Propensity to Cycle 
Tool (PCT) uses national origin-destination datasets with cycling- 
specific routing profiles to identify latent demand and potential health 
and environmental benefits (Lovelace et al., 2017). Methodological 
advances have improved the estimates of cycling potential of this 
approach, including: 
• New network processing and visualisation techniques (Morgan and 
Lovelace, 2020).
• Probabilistic “jittering” of OD data (Lovelace et al., 2022).
• Methods to estimate combined public transport and cycling poten­
tial, with cycling used as the first or last stage of multimodal trips 
(F´elix et al., 2025).
These route and network-based approaches often rely on Open­
StreetMap (OSM) datasets, which have data quality challenges. Sch¨on 
et al. (2024) notes that despite increased OSM usage since 2019, 
inconsistent labeling and quality issues persist. Problems include 
inconsistent road and cycleway classifications, varying data quality, 
update frequencies, and contributor engagement across regions.
Recent algorithms focus on equity and demand sensitivity. 
(Steinacker et al., 2022) showed demand-driven algorithms outperform 
traditional approaches by keeping bike paths on frequently used smaller 
streets and increasing density in high-demand areas. Their adaptive 
inverse percolation framework iteratively removes bike paths based on 
usage patterns, creating networks that better reflect cyclist demand. 
(Hwang et al., 2024) developed methods to prioritise connections to 
marginalised neighbourhoods and transit hubs, showing how connect­
ing bike infrastructure to transit reduces car dependency. Their algo­
rithm prioritises links based on transit access, population density, 
bikeability, and equity to create inclusive mobility solutions. (Mahfouz 
et al., 2023)’s road segment prioritisation model balances direct routes 
with safety and comfort using weighted shortest paths that account for

2.2. Street classification and network hierarchy
Street classification is crucial in urban and transportation planning, 
serving as a vital element for organising the movement of various 
transport modes cohesively and establishing effective road network 
management (Tsigdinos et al., 2024a; Tsigdinos et al., 2024). Tradi­
tional or conventional approaches to street classification typically 
categorise roads based on their emphasis on either movement or local 
access, including fundamental categories such as arterials, collectors, 
and local roads (Marshall, 2004). However, these conventional ap­
proaches have historically prioritised car circulation, often resulting in 
dysfunctional urban areas with significant barriers to active travel 
modes (Appleyard, 1981; Mehaffy et al., 2010). A worldwide review of 
128 national street classification systems found that car-centric hierar­
chies remain globally prevalent, with major arterial roads frequently 
acting as formidable barriers to walking and cycling, particularly when 
they bisect city centres or residential areas (Tsigdinos et al., 2024). This 
fragmentation undermines the continuity and accessibility of active 
travel networks, as users must navigate around high-speed traffic cor­
ridors rather than following direct routes to key destinations (Mehaffy 
et al., 2010).
In response to these limitations, contemporary approaches adopt 
multi-dimensional street classification frameworks balancing movement 
and place functions. The Link and Place paradigm, pioneered by Jones 
and colleagues, assigns each street both a ‘Link’ status (movement 
function) and ‘Place’ status (destination importance), creating matrix- 
based classifications that acknowledge streets as multifunctional 
spaces serving both mobility and social activities (Jones et al., 2007; 
Tsigdinos et al., 2023). This framework has influenced planning in the 
UK, Australia, and Europe. The Netherlands' Sustainable Safety 
approach similarly redefined road categories with explicit cycling ac­
commodations, demonstrating how hierarchical networks can prioritise 
active travel safety (Wegman et al., 2008).
For cycling network design, street classification profoundly affects 
route selection and infrastructure feasibility. Effective networks blend 
low-traffic residential streets for local trips with protected facilities on 
main corridors for cross-town journeys (Furth, 2012). The Level of 
Traffic Stress (LTS) framework categorises roads from LTS 1 (low-stress, 
suitable for children) to LTS 4 (high-speed traffic, experienced riders 
only), enabling planners to develop continuous low-stress networks by 
routing along calm streets or upgrading higher-order roads with pro­
tected facilities, ensuring major corridors do not fragment the cycling 
network (Furth, 2012; Tsigdinos et al., 2023). Recent advances 
demonstrate how multi-dimensional classification can formally elevate 
walking and cycling within street hierarchies, reducing car dominance 
(Tsigdinos et al., 2024b), and provide a foundation for approaches that 
explicitly prioritise sustainable modes while recognising that hierar­
chical networks can still create barriers along major corridors (Mehaffy 
et al., 2010).
Building on these advances, recent approaches emphasise sustain­
able modes like walking, cycling, and public transport, adopting matrix- 
based classification systems that consider both the ‘Link’ function 
(transportation purpose) and the ‘Place’ function (social and cultural 
activities). The hierarchical classification of streets, while organising 
network connectivity, can create challenges for cycling infrastructure 
when major traffic corridors running through central areas become 
significant barriers to movement and disrupt the urban fabric (Mehaffy 
et al., 2010). Understanding this tension is essential for developing 
cycling networks that work within established urban frameworks while 
addressing the needs of cyclists.
For cycling infrastructure planning, street classification influences 
both route selection and design standards, as different road types pre­
sent varying opportunities and constraints for cycling provision. Suc­
cessful cycling networks often utilise a combination of street types, with 
quiet residential streets providing comfortable local access and appro­
priately designed main corridors offering direct connections between 
key destinations. The integration of cycling networks with existing street 
hierarchies therefore requires careful consideration of competing de­
mands for road space and varying levels of motor traffic stress. Recent 
work on sustainable street classification methods that incorporate fac­
tors such as street width, land uses, public transport and cycling routes, 
and street continuity provides valuable frameworks for reformulating 
street networks to prioritise active travel (Tsigdinos et al., 2024a; 
Mehaffy et al., 2010).
2.3. Methodological approaches to cycling network design
Strategic cycling network development ensures sustainable and 
efficient infrastructure investments, with various methodological ap­
proaches emerging to address different aspects of network design.
2.3.1. Percolation and topological approaches
Szell et al. (2022) examined topological constraints and strategic 
planning for cycling network optimisation using percolation theory. 
Their synthetic approach modelled cycling network growth in 62 cities 
including Edinburgh, Leeds, and London, testing three growth strate­
gies: random addition, greedy demand-based expansion, and between­
ness centrality prioritisation. Their findings reveal challenges in creating 
cohesive networks and demonstrate diminishing returns on cycling 
infrastructure investment until reaching critical mass, highlighting the 
need for strategic, persistent investment. Whilst their network expansion 
models show significant overlaps with well-developed cycling networks 
in cities, the synthetic approach using theoretical node placement and 
uniform grid topologies does not directly represent real urban street 
patterns or empirical demand distributions.
2.3.2. Demand-driven and route choice approaches
Steinacker et al. (2022) advanced the field by developing calibrated 
cyclist route choice models using bike-sharing trip data to predict 
ridership potential. Their methodology employs penalty factors for 
different street types reflecting cyclist preferences, with iterative 
network expansion prioritising segments based on demand-weighted 
routing. Applied across multiple cities using OpenStreetMap topology 
and empirical bike-share demand patterns, this approach demonstrates 
how networks can be optimised for actual usage patterns. However, 
reliance on bike-sharing data introduces context-specific limitations, as 
these usage patterns may not generalise to broader cycling populations 
and require data infrastructure available only in select cities.
2.3.3. Equity-aware and complete streets methods
Hwang et al. (2024) focused on connecting fragmented existing 
cycling infrastructure using three distinct path-finding methods: short­
est path (geometric optimisation), least cost path (multi-criteria 
including traffic, slope, infrastructure quality), and demand-weighted 
routing. Their approach integrates equity metrics and transit connec­
tivity analysis, emphasising Complete Streets principles and multi- 
modal integration whilst targeting schools, transit stations, employ­
ment centres, and parks as priority destinations.
Mahfouz et al. (2023)
implemented a multi-stage approach 
combining gravity-based demand modelling, Level of Traffic Stress 
(LTS) routing, and community detection for equitable infrastructure 
allocation. Comparing utilitarian expansion (maximising total ridership) 
with egalitarian expansion (proportional distribution across commu­
nities), their framework explicitly addresses trade-offs between effi­
ciency and spatial equity in network development.

2.4. Network evaluation tools
To address the lack of standardised evaluation metrics, Grisiute et al. 
(2024) introduced the VeloNEMO ontology. This framework harmonises 
diverse bike network evaluation metrics by defining relationships be­
tween metrics and evaluation components. VeloNEMO classifies 176 
distinct metric types across 28 evaluation criteria, capturing attributes 
like metrics, criteria, methods, and data sources. Common metrics 
include Slope, IntersectionDensity, DestinationDensity, and BikeLane­
Type, often associated with Accessibility and Attractiveness. However, 
current frameworks struggle with Space Syntax metrics and dis­
tinguishing between objective and subjective evaluations.
User behaviour studies emphasise designing cycling networks that 
meet user needs and preferences. Pikora et al. (2003) created a frame­
work to assess environmental determinants of active travel, highlighting 
objective factors (road width, traffic speeds) and subjective perceptions 
(safety, aesthetics) affecting cycling willingness. They found street­
scapes, trees, maintenance, cleanliness, parks, and path continuity 
contribute to route attractiveness. Similarly, Buehler and Dill (2016b)
showed connected, low-stress bikeway networks significantly influence 
uptake by reducing perceived danger and inconvenience.
Context-sensitive planning and stakeholder engagement are 
increasingly recognised in network design. Portuguese case studies by 
Fonseca et al. (2023) show how factors like population density, age, 
slopes, street hierarchy, and proximity to community facilities should 
inform network design, especially in cities beginning to develop cycling 
infrastructure. These considerations are crucial for “starter cities” with 
low cycling rates.
2.5. Economic efficiency and optimisation
Economic analysis of cycling infrastructure investment provides a 
crucial dimension to network planning. Paulsen and Rich (2024) and 
Paulsen and Rich (2023) developed frameworks for evaluating the cost- 
effectiveness of cycling infrastructure interventions, showing how stra­
tegic network investments can yield substantial economic returns 
through health benefits, reduced congestion, and lower emissions. 
Similarly, Bonsma-Fisher et al. (2024) demonstrated optimisation 
techniques that maximise network utility while respecting budgetary 
constraints, helping planners identify the most efficient allocation of 
limited resources across competing infrastructure needs.
2.6. Research gaps and future directions
Despite progress in cycling infrastructure development, several 
critical gaps remain: 
• Integration of multiple data sources: Methods for systematically 
combining authoritative base maps with crowdsourced data and 
demand estimates remain underdeveloped, limiting network gener­
ation quality and coverage.
• Stakeholder engagement: Current approaches treat stakeholder 
input as post-hoc consultation rather than integrated throughout the 
design process, neglecting practitioner knowledge and user prefer­
ences essential for deliverable infrastructure.
• Policy and implementation alignment: Tools rarely account for 
policy compliance requirements, jurisdictional constraints, or 
phased funding realities, producing networks that are theoretically 
optimal but practically undeliverable.
• Demand-driven network generation: Existing methods either rely 
on synthetic demand assumptions or require specialised data (e.g., 
bike-sharing records) unavailable in most contexts, limiting appli­
cability for evidence-based planning.
These gaps highlight the need for integrated methodology combining 
diverse data sources with stakeholder requirements while adapting to 
local policy contexts and implementation constraints. Our corenet 
methodology directly addresses these gaps by: (1) systematically inte­
grating multiple data sources (OS, OSM, and cycling potential estimates) 
to overcome data quality limitations, (2) incorporating stakeholder 
engagement at multiple decision points throughout the process, (3) 
aligning with established policy frameworks through parameter selec­
tion based on design guidelines, and (4) employing demand-driven 
network generation using widely-available census-based cycling po­
tential data rather than specialised datasets.

3. Data
Development of a core cycling network requires accurate and 
comprehensive input data. This section describes the datasets employed, 
identifies key barriers to complete network generation, and presents the 
hybrid approach adopted to address these challenges.
3.1. Input data
The corenet workflow synthesises four complementary datasets: 
• Ordnance Survey Open Roads (OS) provides a reliable represen­
tation of Great Britain's road network with standardised road clas­
sification, high positional accuracy, and simplified geometry suited 
to transportation planning. Limitations include restricted geographic 
coverage (UK-only), quarterly update frequency, and minimal 
cycling-specific infrastructure information.
• OpenStreetMap (OSM) offers detailed, versatile geographic data 
with rich attributes including cycling infrastructure, global coverage, 
and frequent community updates. However, OSM presents chal­
lenges with inconsistent quality across regions, complex geometry 
requiring substantial processing, and non-standardised road 
classifications.
• Propensity to Cycle Tool (PCT) provides cycling demand estimates 
derived from census-based origin-destination matrices that explicitly 
account for infrastructure-induced demand through scenario 
modelling (Lovelace et al., 2017). The influence network Ginfluence is 
populated using PCT outputs for the Go Dutch scenario, which 
models cycling uptake rates equivalent to those observed in the 
Netherlands, a country with comprehensive cycling infrastructure 
and high cycling participation, for trips of equivalent distance and 
hilliness (Lovelace et al., 2017; Goodman et al., 2019). This scenario- 
based approach addresses the infrastructure-demand feedback loop 
by estimating cycling potential under conditions of high-quality 
infrastructure provision, rather than extrapolating from current 
(often infrastructure-limited) cycling levels in the UK (Buehler and 
Dill, 2016a). The Go Dutch scenario incorporates distance decay 
functions calibrated on Dutch cycling behaviour, effectively model­
ling how comprehensive cycling infrastructure influences trip mode 
choice across different journey lengths (Lovelace et al., 2017). PCT 
combines these scenario-adjusted origin-destination flows with 
routing profiles accounting for distance, hilliness, and route direct­
ness, producing link-level demand estimates f(e) that represent po­
tential rather than current demand. These estimates are then 
projected onto the OS base network to identify corridors with highest 
cycling potential under improved infrastructure conditions.

3.2. Barriers to complete cycling network generation
Whilst each dataset provides valuable information, analysis reveals 
three critical barriers when using either OS or OSM data alone for 
cycling network generation: 
1. Connectivity Issues: OSM data contains topological errors, partic­
ularly at junctions between different network types (e.g., footways or 
cycleways not properly connected to roads). Whilst OS data provides 
superior network integrity with consistently connected segments, 
relying solely on OS data excludes crucial off-road cycling 
infrastructure.
2. Road Classification Inconsistency: OSM applies multiple classifi­
cation schemes to the same road segment (e.g., Princes Street is 
variably classified as “tertiary,” “unclassified,” and “primary”). 
Although intended to provide detailed information, this complicates 
network planning based on road type. OS data offers consistent and 
standardised classifications aligned with official transport planning 
categories, but lacks the rich attribute detail needed to identify 
cycling-friendly routes.
3. Off-Road Path Coverage: OSM provides comprehensive mapping of 
cycling infrastructure including park trails and off-street paths, 
whereas OS data focuses primarily on road networks with minimal 
cycling-specific features. A complete cycling network requires both 
on-road and off-road infrastructure.
Appendix A provides comprehensive examination of these differ­
ences with supporting figures and examples.
3.3. Hybrid approach to address data barriers
Given these complementary strengths and limitations, the method­
ology addresses these three barriers by integrating data sources strate­
gically. OS data serves as the base network (Gbase) due to its topological 
reliability and standardised road classification, resolving the connec­
tivity and classification inconsistency issues. OSM contributes detailed 
cycling infrastructure information, particularly off-road paths, 
addressing the coverage barrier. This hybrid approach combines the rich 
infrastructure detail of OSM with the structural integrity of OS data, 
enabling complete cycling network generation that includes both on- 
road and off-road infrastructure (Fig. 1).

4. Methodology of Core cycling network generation
This section outlines our systematic approach to generating core 
cycling networks that integrate transport agency requirements with 
empirical cycling potential data, origin-destination travel patterns, and 
network topology. While the method uses graph theory concepts for 
network analysis, it is fundamentally driven by travel demand data and 
policy objectives rather than network structure alone. A critical meth­
odological consideration concerns the nature of the demand data 
employed: the cycling potential estimates represent modelled future 
demand under improved cycling conditions, not current cycling vol­
umes. This approach specifically addresses the circular causality chal­
lenge in cycling infrastructure planning, whereby low cycling levels can 
result from inadequate infrastructure whilst infrastructure investment 
typically responds to observed demand. Rather than basing networks on 
existing (often limited) cycling activity, we identify routes with the 
highest potential for cycling uptake if appropriate infrastructure were 
provided. This forward-looking approach enables the identification of 
strategic corridors where cycling should ideally occur, supporting the

4.1. Network generation workflow
The methodology has two main stages: (1) preparing the base layer 
for network development and (2) generating the core cycling network. 
Table 3 summarises key variables and parameters used.
The methodology employs graph theory terminology that corre­
sponds directly to standard planning concepts: ‘edges’ represent road 
segments or links in the network, ‘nodes’ represent junctions or in­
tersections, ‘dangles’ represent dead-end segments that terminate 
without connecting to other parts of the network, ‘clustering’ represents 
grouping nearby locations into coherent areas or corridors, and ‘shortest 
paths’ represent the most direct routes between destinations.
5. Network preparation and data integration
In this step, cycling potential attribute fe from the influence network 
Ginfluence is joined to each edge of the base network Gbase. Two functions 
can achieve this: stplanr::rnet_merge and corenet::anime_join (which 
relies on the anime package). After merging, the base network is filtered 
to include only specified road-type classes troad. This ensures the core 
network contains roads that align with policy preferences. At this stage 
the OS Open Roads geometry (defining Gbase) is combined with PCT- 
derived demand values f(e), ensuring that subsequent filtering retains 
the road classes that Scottish local authorities are empowered to 
reconfigure.
6. High-potential segment selection
To identify high cycling potential road segments, key attribute f(e) is 
evaluated for each edge. Edges exceeding threshold θthres are selected as 
high-potential segments:

The corenet methodology strategically focuses on A and B roads, 
which are under local authority jurisdiction and typically possess suf­
ficient carriageway width for cycling infrastructure implementation. 
This focus was informed by consultation with transport planning pro­
fessionals from Transport Scotland and Sustrans Scotland, who 
emphasised the importance of targeting routes where authorities have 
implementation powers and where space constraints are more 
manageable. Street width analysis conducted using the NPT ‘street­
space’ layer, which classifies roads based on their capacity to accom­
modate four segregated cycling infrastructure scenarios: (1) 2 × 1-way 
tracks within road width, (2) 2 × 1-way tracks using edge-to-edge, (3) 2- 
way track within road width, and (4) 2-way track using edge-to-edge, 
under three categories: ‘Not enough space,’ ‘Absolute minimum,’ and 
‘Desirable minimum’, confirms that A and B roads generally possess 
adequate space for high-quality cycling provision. This strategic tar­
geting ensures that generated networks prioritise routes that are both 
high-demand and physically feasible for infrastructure delivery, 
addressing implementation constraints at the network selection stage 
rather than as a post-processing filter.
7. Clustering of candidate points
A density-based spatial clustering algorithm (DBSCAN) with prox­
imity threshold ϵ merges nearby points along corridors or intersections 
into clusters (Ester et al., 1996). This approach is well-suited for iden­
tifying coherent cycling corridors as it can handle irregular spatial dis­
tributions and does not require pre-specification of cluster numbers 
(Birant and Kut, 2007). This produces clusters C1, C2, …, CK representing 
potential core network nodes. To capture each corridor's full extent, 
clusters are grouped by road name or other identifier. For each group, 
key representative points (endpoints and geometric centroid) are 
selected as core network nodes. These points P = {p1, p2, …, pM} serve as 
anchors along high-potential routes for network connection. Because 
these segments inherit OD-informed demand from the PCT network, the 
resulting anchor set P explicitly tracks corridors with strong observed 
commuting flows.

10.1. Parameter selection aligned with CbD best practice
CbD set core cycling network standards to promote active travel, 
building on UK DfT guidance, particularly Cycle Infrastructure Design 
(LTN 1/20) (Department for Transport, 2020). Our methodology meets 
these requirements through careful parameter selection and user feed­
back. By focusing on directness, accessibility, and route cohesion, we 
refine parameters like road classification scoring, arterial weighting, and 
mesh density to address practical needs and stakeholder insights as 
specified in these policy documents. This creates networks aligned with 
CbD & other network planning best practice. The following subsections 
show how these refinements ensure connectivity, accommodate varying 
population densities, and maintain cohesive structure in accordance 
with established policy frameworks.
10.2. Network quality criteria and methodological focus
CbD establishes several key network quality criteria: directness 
(minimising detours), safety and comfort (appropriate segregation and 
design), coherence and connectivity (joined-up networks), attractive­
ness (pleasant environment), accessibility (serving diverse users and

Journal of Transport Geography 131 (2026) 104549

destinations), and integration with other transport modes. Additional 
considerations include gradient management, surface quality, lighting, 
maintenance standards, and equitable provision across different 
communities.
Our corenet methodology prioritises a focused subset of these criteria 
based on data availability and computational feasibility: directness 
(through arterial weighting and shortest-path algorithms), connectivity 
and coherence (via network growth strategies and systematic removal 
of disconnected segments), coverage and accessibility (through spatial 
analysis and buffer zone evaluation), and demand alignment (by 
integrating cycling potential data to focus on high-usage corridors). 
These criteria align with the core design principles established in 
Cycling by Design (Transport Scotland, 2021), which emphasises that 
cycling infrastructure should be direct (offering the most direct route, 
minimising detours), coherent (forming continuous networks linking 
origins and destinations), and accessible (serving diverse communities 
and destinations).
This focused approach reflects the limitations of automated network 
generation using available datasets, which cannot directly capture 
subjective factors like perceived safety, environmental attractiveness, or 
comfort levels without extensive field surveys or user research. How­
ever, by establishing a robust foundation addressing the quantifiable 
core criteria, the methodology provides a starting point for further 
refinement incorporating local knowledge and qualitative assessments 
during stakeholder engagement phases. Future iterations could integrate 
additional data sources (e.g., safety records, environmental quality 
indices, accessibility audits) to expand the range of criteria addressed 
algorithmically.
10.3. Hierarchical road prioritisation and arterial weighting
CbD prioritises that route selection should be both intuitive and 
direct, with an emphasis on high usage potential. To achieve these ob­
jectives, a hierarchical decision framework is implemented to refine 
route selection when multiple segments exhibit comparable cycling 
potential or similar classification scores. Specifically: 
1. Routes with higher cycling potential are prioritised.
2. When cycling potential is comparable, “A Road” segments are chosen 
over “B Road” segments, which in turn are chosen over “Minor Road” 
segments.
3. When cycling potential values are identical, the network favours 
routes associated with higher-ranked road types.
4. If both cycling potential values and road classifications are equiva­
lent, the shortest route is selected.
This framework is operationalised through the parameters road_­
function, road_scores, and Arterialness. Preferred road types are desig­
nated with scores (e.g. “A Road” = 1, “B Road” = 10, and “Minor Road” 
= 100) where lower scores indicate higher priority. The value of 
Arterialness quantifies the significance of each route segment by 
emphasising those with greater cycling potential and directness. A cubic 
power formula employed to accentuate high-value segments, and a 
balanced weighting scheme integrates arterial importance with road 
classification. This systematic approach ensures that, among parallel 
routes with similar cycling potential, “A Road” segments are preferen­
tially selected, thereby enhancing both network effectiveness and 
directness.
10.4. Adaptive mesh density calibration via DBSCAN clustering
To address population density considerations and meet guidelines, 
we employ DBSCAN clustering. By adjusting the maximum neighbour­
hood radius (ϵ) and minimum points required for clusters (MinPts), we 
precisely control network mesh density. In urban centres, a smaller 
radius (250 m) creates higher route density, meeting the 250-m mesh 
target in dense areas. In suburban areas, a larger radius (400 m) creates 
lower route density, meeting the 400-m target in less populated regions. 
This tailored approach ensures appropriate network density based on 
population concentration.
10.5. Enhancing network cohesion and connectivity
Establishing a cohesive network depends on robust connectivity and 
the systematic removal of fragmented segments. We conduct compre­
hensive connectivity analysis to identify and remove isolated segments, 
ensuring seamless route integration. Furthermore, the iterative removal 
of edges terminating at degree-one nodes (dangling edges) can be cus­
tomised, allowing users to specify the desired level of network refine­
ment. This flexibility enables the network to be pruned according to user 
preferences, resulting in a final configuration that is both well- 
connected and accessible, thereby effectively promoting active travel.
10.6. Network growth
Network growth can be controlled by adjusting the npt_threshold 
parameter in corenet::corenet(), which influences road selection based 
on cycling potential. Higher thresholds select only highest-demand 
segments, while lower thresholds include more roads, expanding 
coverage. Examining network evolution across threshold levels provides 
insights into potential expansion areas.
Fig. 2 shows Edinburgh's core cycling network at four cycling po­
tential thresholds (99th to 94th percentiles). Higher thresholds isolate 
critical corridors, forming the network's backbone by connecting major 
employment, transport, and residential hubs; routes offering maximum 
utility with minimal investment. In Edinburgh, this core comprises 
central arterial routes and key district connections, typical of radial 
urban demand.
This visualisation reveals several important strategic planning in­
sights that extend beyond simple network expansion. The highest 
threshold (99th percentile) identifies the fundamental network skeleton: 
the absolutely critical corridors that form the backbone of any effective 
cycling network. As thresholds decrease to the 97th and 95th percen­
tiles, the network exhibits strategic densification around this core, with 
new segments extending existing corridors and creating parallel route 
options in high-demand areas. The transition to the 94th percentile 
shows the network beginning to extend into moderate-density residen­
tial areas, creating a more comprehensive system that serves diverse trip 
purposes beyond commuting. This stage represents the point where the 
network evolves from serving primarily high-demand commuter routes 
to providing a more equitable system that accommodates leisure 
cycling, school journeys, and local access needs.

11.1. KPI selection
The evaluation metrics presented in this section were chosen to 
address fundamental requirements: alignment with established network 
quality criteria from cycling infrastructure guidance, operational feasi­
bility given available data sources, and comprehensive assessment of 
network performance across multiple dimensions critical to cycling 
uptake.
11.2. Alignment with established network quality criteria
Our metric selection builds directly on the core network quality 
criteria established in CbD, which consistently identify coherence and 
connectivity, directness, accessibility, safety and comfort, and 
attractiveness as fundamental network qualities that determine cycling 
infrastructure effectiveness.
Four of our metric categories directly operationalise these estab­
lished criteria. Network Cohesion and Connectivity directly measures 
the coherence criterion, evaluating whether infrastructure forms joined- 
up networks rather than isolated segments. Directness and Efficiency 
translates the directness criterion into quantifiable measures that assess 
route circuity and network-level efficiency. Origin-Destination 
Accessibility operationalises the accessibility criterion by measuring 
how well networks serve actual and potential cycling trips between 
specific locations. Demand-Aligned Coverage complements this by 
evaluating whether the network is positioned in areas with high cycling 
potential and population density, ensuring infrastructure investment 
targets the areas of greatest need.
The remaining two criteria: safety/comfort and attractiveness, pose 
significant challenges for automated assessment using available data­
sets. These subjective qualities require detailed infrastructure audits, 
perception surveys, or environmental assessments that extend beyond 
the scope of automated network generation. Our approach acknowl­
edges this limitation while focusing on the quantifiable criteria that can 
be systematically evaluated across different urban contexts and network 
designs.
11.3. Integration with planning practice and decision-making
The metric framework is designed to support iterative network 
design and strategic decision-making rather than simply providing ac­
ademic assessment tools. Each metric addresses specific questions that 
planners and transport authorities regularly face: 
• Network Cohesion metrics answer “Are we creating joined-up 
infrastructure or isolated segments?” and support decisions about 
connection priorities and phasing strategies.
• Demand-Aligned Coverage metrics answer “Are we investing in 
the right places?” and guide prioritisation decisions when funding is 
limited.
• O–D Accessibility metrics answer “Who does this network serve?” 
and support equity analysis and stakeholder engagement.
• Directness and Efficiency metrics answer “How circuitous are 
network routes and can users travel efficiently between destina­
tions?” and inform design decisions about route selection and 
network connectivity.
This decision-focused approach ensures that evaluation serves 
planning rather than purely academic purposes, supporting the meth­
odology's objective of producing actionable rather than merely 
descriptive network assessments.

Fig. 4 shows the core and existing cycling networks for four Scottish 
cities. Fig. 5 illustrates key metrics for these cities, with each chart 
presenting performance indicators that compare the generated core 
cycling network to the existing cycling infrastructure. Higher values 
indicate better performance.
The radar charts display seven key performance metrics: Spatial 
Coverage, Zone Connectivity, Cycling Potential Coverage, Density Ratio, 
Population Coverage, OD Accessibility, and OD Coverage (both count- 
based and demand-weighted). Four additional metrics: Directness, 
Global Efficiency, Local Efficiency, and Routing Success Rate, are pre­
sented separately in Table 4 rather than on the radar chart axes.
This design choice addresses two limitations. First, efficiency values 
vary substantially between cities, making them unsuitable for radar 
visualisation where consistent scaling is essential. Second, whilst most 
radar chart metrics are standardised so that values approaching 1.0 
represent optimal performance, the directness and efficiency metrics use 
different scaling interpretations. Including them on the same axes would 
distort the comparative presentation and make the charts difficult to 
interpret.
Comparative analysis of both radar chart metrics (Fig. 5) and effi­
ciency measures (Table 4) reveals substantial differences between pro­
posed core networks and existing infrastructure across all four cities.
As shown in Fig. 5, the core networks (highlighted in red on the radar 
charts) exhibit stronger performance in Cycling Potential Coverage and 
Density Ratio, suggesting better alignment with travel demand and more 
comprehensive spatial coverage. Similarly, the metrics in Table 4
demonstrate substantial gains in network efficiency: directness im­
proves by a factor of 4 to 25, global efficiency increases by 13 to 188 
times, local efficiency improves 5- to 6-fold, and routing success rates 
rise from below 1% in existing networks to between 3.6% and 59.6% in 
core networks. These results underscore the fragmented nature of cur­
rent cycling infrastructure, where the most dramatic improvements are 
observed. Core networks overcome this limitation by establishing 
cohesive, well-connected systems that support practical, continuous 
cycling journeys across urban areas.
12. Discussion
The methods presented in this paper generate data-driven and scal­
able networks to support investment in the joined-up networks that are 
needed for cost-effective investment in cycling infrastructure. An 
important aspect is its flexibility: parameters determining the nature of 
the networks generated were adjusted following consultation with 
planners and the approach can be used in other contexts. This approach 
allows the results to reflect the preferences of diverse user groups, 
enhancing public acceptance and shared ownership of cycling initia­
tives, opening up the possibility of citizen-led design (Lovelace et al., 
2020). The approach adds value to methods for current and future po­
tential demand estimation (Paulsen and Rich, 2024; 2023; F´elix et al., 
2025; Lovelace et al., 2017) by highlighting core networks. Incorpo­
rating diverse datasets (in this case from official Ordnance Survey and 
crowd-sourced OpenStreetMap sources) enables a ‘best of both worlds’ 
approach that tackles the challenges related to data quality and 
completeness. Aligning the methodology with overarching transport 
policies, including those advocated by Transport Scotland, ensures it 
contributes to broader social, health, environmental, and economic 
goals. The methods are available in an open-source package, providing a 
new tool for planners and researchers to create policy-aligned networks.
While the methodology has many strengths, it faces notable chal­
lenges, particularly in ensuring data quality. OpenStreetMap provides 
detailed and frequently updated information, but its open tagging sys­
tem introduces complexities that can compromise data reliability. The 
flexibility of this system, while advantageous, often results in inconsis­
tent or incorrect tagging, leading to errors of omission and commission

cycling networks (Fleischmann et al., 2025).
Incorporating qualitative data, such as perceived safety or comfort, 
remains a further area for improvement. While stakeholder feedback can 
be included by changing parameters following feedback or during 
workshops with stakeholders, developing systematic approaches to 
capture these subjective dimensions could refine network designs and 
improve uptake. Generalisability is similarly a concern. Although the 
methodology was developed with a Scottish context in mind, its broader 
applicability to regions with differing policy, demographic, or 
geographical features has yet to be tested. Finally, merging multiple data 
sources to obtain richer detail increases the complexity of filtering and 
validation, highlighting the need for established workflows that main­
tain data accuracy.
An additional consideration concerns the relationship between 
infrastructure provision, demand, and the wider built-environment 
context. Cycling infrastructure induces additional trips whilst inade­
quate infrastructure suppresses demand (Buehler and Dill, 2016a; 
Goodman et al., 2019). The methodology addresses this through two 
mechanisms: (1) PCT's Go Dutch scenario models potential under high- 
quality infrastructure rather than current infrastructure-constrained 
conditions, providing a vision-led basis for strategic planning, and (2) 
existing cycling infrastructure from OSM influences routing and demand 
estimates, allowing networks to be regenerated as infrastructure evolves 
and is mapped. However, the current implementation does not include 
automated iterative feedback where demand is re-estimated after each 
hypothetical network addition during the planning phase, nor does it 
capture ‘network effects’ where each link disproportionately increases 
utility by creating new connections (Szell et al., 2022). This represents a 
practical simplification for computational tractability in strategic plan­
ning contexts with long timescales.
Beyond infrastructure, built environment attributes also influence 
cycling uptake. PCT estimates inherently incorporate population den­
sity, employment centres, and topography (Lovelace et al., 2017), but do 
not explicitly model street permeability (Handy et al., 2002), land use 
mix (Cervero and Kockelman, 1997), traffic volumes (Winters et al.,

Future research directions include: (1) developing dynamic network 
growth models that iteratively re-estimate demand after each addition 
(Creutzig et al., 2020); (2) integrating additional built environment data 
including traffic stress indices (Mekuria et al., 2012) and proximity to 
attractors; (3) calibrating behavioural models for heterogeneous re­
sponses across demographic groups (Heinen et al., 2010); and (4) 
establishing monitoring frameworks for ‘plan-build-monitor-adapt’ cy­
cles using observed uptake (Aldred et al., 2019). These enhancements 
would strengthen infrastructure-demand-environment interactions 
whilst maintaining practical applicability for strategic planning.
In addition to these research avenues, practical, implementation- 
oriented development priorities include:
12.1. Network customisation
Currently, the automated network generation process limits direct 
user alterations. An interactive editing feature would be beneficial, 
allowing stakeholders to tailor the final network to specific local con­
ditions and integrate unique needs and preferences into the design.
12.2. Generation speed
While core network generation speed has been optimised signifi­
cantly, producing a city-scale network for a large urban area like Edin­
burgh still takes approximately 15 min. Further improvements in 
processing time are desirable to facilitate faster iteration cycles for 
planners, enabling more rapid scenario testing and agile decision- 
making.
12.3. Additional factors
Integrating more information on road safety, rider comfort, road 
width, the availability of local amenities, and the type and quality of 
cycle infrastructure could better align networks with user preferences, 
ultimately supporting more effective and user-centric infrastructure 
planning. Advanced data collection techniques, such as crowdsourcing 
and real-time sensors, could provide timely behavioural insights. 
Interdisciplinary collaboration with experts in health, urban design, and 
social sciences would also ensure a more comprehensive understanding 
of cycling's impacts on diverse populations.
12.4. Implementation constraints
The current methodology does not directly operationalise physical 
implementation constraints such as street width, available space for 
cycling infrastructure, or engineering feasibility. These factors are 
crucial for determining whether proposed routes can be practically 
delivered; in NPT, a ‘streetspace’ layer classifies corridors by ability to 
accommodate segregated provision. Future versions will integrate this 
layer and related datasets (street width measurements, land use con­
straints, and infrastructure cost estimates) into the generation pipeline. 
For now, feasibility is screened with streetspace during parameter se­
lection and addressed at detailed design or through local knowledge in 
stakeholder engagement.
12.5. Land use integration
Our current methodology focuses primarily on travel demand pat­
terns and network connectivity on road types where local authorities 
have implementation rights (A roads, B roads, and minor roads), rather 
than residential streets. This strategic focus ensures the resulting net­
works prioritise routes that can be realistically implemented by trans­
port authorities. While our approach does not explicitly incorporate land 
use data or POI, integrating such information could enhance network 
planning by ensuring connections to key destinations such as schools, 
employment centres, retail areas, and recreational facilities. This could 
be achieved by: (1) weighting cycling potential estimates based on 
proximity to important land uses, (2) incorporating POI data as addi­
tional anchor points in the clustering process, (3) using land use density 
as a factor in determining appropriate network mesh density, and (4) 
considering mixed-use development patterns when setting connectivity 
requirements. However, our current focus on higher-order roads (those 
under local authority jurisdiction) already tends to connect major ac­
tivity centres and employment hubs, as these roads typically serve as the 
primary corridors linking key destinations within urban areas. Such 
integration of explicit land use data would support more holistic trans­
port and urban planning approaches, ensuring that cycling networks 
serve the full range of urban activities and support sustainable devel­
opment patterns.
12.6. Multiple network scenarios
While this paper demonstrates the generation of a single core 
network per study area, the methodology supports the creation of 
multiple planning alternatives through parameter variation. Different 
threshold levels (θthres) naturally generate networks of varying density 
and coverage, as shown in Fig. 2 for Edinburgh. Additionally, alternative 
scenarios could be created by: (1) varying road type preferences to 
explore different design philosophies, (2) adjusting clustering parame­
ters to test different mesh densities, (3) incorporating different policy 
priorities through modified weighting schemes, and (4) examining 
budget-constrained scenarios by limiting network extent. Such multi- 
scenario analysis would enable planners to evaluate trade-offs be­
tween coverage, connectivity, and implementation feasibility, support­
ing more robust decision-making processes.

12.7. Equity and accessibility
Ensuring equitable resource distribution and access for all commu­
nity segments remains essential. A city's network requirements may 
evolve with changes in population density, urban development, and 
emerging technologies like e-bikes and smart infrastructure. Enabling 
dynamic network adaptation in response to these shifts would enhance 
long-term resilience. Future research could also focus on qualitative 
metrics, such as perceived safety or convenience, to refine current 
evaluation standards and capture the nuanced factors influencing 
cycling uptake.
Overall, advancing the approach through increased flexibility, more 
efficient processing, and expanded functionality could accelerate its 
adoption by practitioners. Comparative studies with other network 
design methods could illuminate best practices and guide iterative re­
finements, further improving cycling infrastructure and bolstering its 
role in sustainable urban transport systems.
13. Conclusion
This paper introduces the corenet method, a flexible and data-driven 
approach to address key challenges in active travel planning. By inte­
grating predictive analytics, infrastructure evaluation, and stakeholder 
feedback, our methodology offers significant contributions to sustain­
able transport planning. Key contributions include: 
1. Methodological Innovation: The corenet framework merges mul­
tiple data sources (NPT cycling data, Ordnance Survey Open Roads, 
and OpenStreetMap) and analytical techniques to create cohesive, 
policy-compliant cycling networks, effectively tackling connectivity 
and classification challenges.
2. Practical Implementation: The open-source package makes this 
methodology accessible to transport planners and researchers. Its 
application in Scotland demonstrates real-world effectiveness, pro­
ducing networks with improved connectivity, coverage, and policy 
alignment.
3. Stakeholder Integration: Emphasising local knowledge and user 
feedback throughout the planning process ensures that networks 
meet community needs and expectations, fostering greater accep­
tance and promoting sustainable travel behaviour.
4. Comprehensive Evaluation: We have developed a robust set of 
metrics for assessing network performance across multiple di­
mensions, equipping planners with tools to identify strengths and 
weaknesses in proposed or existing cycling infrastructure.
Findings from our Scottish case studies demonstrate that targeted 
data preprocessing, calibrated parameters, and prioritisation of high- 
demand corridors can produce networks finely tuned to local condi­
tions, effectively balancing objectives such as cohesion, coverage, and 
directness. By offering more direct and convenient routes, the corenet 
approach can encourage a shift from motorised travel towards cycling. 
Ultimately, by combining empirical evidence, policy guidance, and 
ongoing collaboration with local communities, this methodology 
bridges the gap between theoretical best practices and on-the-ground 
implementation. As cities worldwide increasingly prioritise active 
travel, the corenet framework offers an evidence-based, adaptable 
method for developing cycling infrastructure that is both practical and 
responsive to local needs. This contributes to building healthier, more 
resilient, and sustainable urban transport systems globally.

The development of a core cycling network relies on accurate and comprehensive data sources that capture urban transport system complexity. 
This section details the primary data sources used: Ordnance Survey Open Roads (OS) and OpenStreetMap (OSM). We discuss their strengths and 
limitations and explain how they influence cycling network creation.
A.1. Ordnance Survey Open Roads (OS)
OS provides a reliable, detailed representation of Great Britain's road network with several key advantages:

• High Accuracy and Consistency: Maintained by professional surveyors, providing a reliable foundation for transport network analysis.
• Standardised Road Classification: Roads classified by national standards, simplifying integration with other government planning tools.
• Simplified Representation: Uses single central lines for road carriageways, simplifying network visualisation and analysis.

• Restricted Geographic Coverage: Focuses exclusively on Great Britain.
• Limited Update Frequency: May not capture rapid changes in road configurations or new infrastructure quickly.
• Lack of Cycling Path Information: Often lacks detailed information on dedicated cycling paths.
A.2. OpenStreetMap (OSM)
OpenStreetMap provides detailed, versatile geographic data valuable for cycling network planning: 
• Rich Detail: Offers lane-level detail with extensive attributes including cycle lanes, barriers, and surface types essential for effective network 
planning.
• Community Updates: As a crowdsourced dataset, OSM updates continuously, often providing more current data than traditional sources. New 
infrastructure appears quickly - for example, active travel bridges at Methilhill and Cameron Bridge completed in September 2024 were in OSM by 
November 2024.
• Global Coverage: Worldwide scope enables international comparisons and cross-border studies.
However, OSM presents challenges: 
• Inconsistent Quality: Data quality varies by region and contributor expertise, requiring careful validation.
• Complex Processing: The data richness requires sophisticated filtering to extract relevant information for network planning.
A.3. Data richness comparison between OS and OSM
As shown in Fig. 6, OSM data has significantly higher geometric complexity than OS data. This complexity comes from OSM's inclusion of detailed 
information on roads, railways, waterways, paths, cycleways, and various attributes (road surface, speed limits, pedestrian facilities). For example, at 
Princes Street, OSM shows lane-level information and intricate intersections, while OS data offers a more generalised representation.
While OSM's detail allows for flexible use cases, it increases processing complexity. In contrast, OS data provides a structured, simplified dataset 
focused on roads, making it ideal for transportation planning. As a government dataset, it ensures uniform road representation with official classi­
fications. Transport agencies often prefer this simplicity when focusing on network structure rather than detailed attributes.
