Shear Behavior Model for FRP-Confined
and Unconfined Rubberized Concrete
Zhao Wang1; Lin Chen2; Maurizio Guadagnini3; and Kypros Pilakoutas4
Abstract: This paper presents the experimental and numerical results of an ongoing investigation aimed at developing high-strength,
high-deformability fiber-reinforced polymer (FRP) confined rubberized concrete (CRuC) suitable for structural applications. The rubberized
concrete (RuC) utilizes recycled rubber particles as a replacement for both fine and coarse aggregates. Rubber aggregates reduce the
compressive strength and stiffness of RuC, thus limiting its application for structural purposes. Confining RuC with FRP jackets recovers
strength and enables the development of high deformability, ductility, and energy dissipation capacity. Recent research mainly focuses on the
axial performance of RuC and CRuC, but little work exists on the shear behavior of this flexible concretes. This paper adopts a nonlinear
numerical approach for the practical implementation of the smeared, fixed-angle crack approach in finite element analysis to predict the shear
response of RuC and CRuC. Constitutive models are proposed on the basis of fundamental test results. The model is validated through a
simulation of a series of shear tests on RuC and CRuC with different shear span-to-depth ratios (a=d). The model predictions are then
compared against the experimental results, and good agreement is found. DOI: 10.1061/(ASCE)CC.1943-5614.0000962. © 2019 American
Society of Civil Engineers.

Introduction
Elements of large reinforced concrete (RC) structures can be sub-
jected to significant lateral loads and/or deformations from either
shrinkage and thermal moments or accidental actions, such as those
arising from differential settlement or seismic motion. To prevent
the development of large moments, highly deformable elements
often made with elastomeric materials are utilized, such as rubber
bridge bearings or base isolation units. These elements are required
to carry high axial loads and simultaneously be sufficiently flexible
to allow large shear deformations. In general, these elements are not
as durable as the structure itself and, hence, require inspection and
regular replacement, adding costs and service disruptions. Conven-
tional RC elements deflect mainly through flexural deformations
and offer relatively high shear stiffness. Thus, the development
of highly flexible RC elements that can lead to integrated structures
can have a strong impact on RC structural design. To achieve this
goal, more flexible concrete materials need to be developed and
rubberized concrete (RuC) is a natural candidate.
Rubber extracted from tires is a highly durable material, and it
has good strength, flexibility, and a remarkable ability to maintain
its volume under stress. As a result, there is a drive to find new
applications for rubber and, in particular, for waste rubber extracted
from scrap tires. In the past few decades, the use of rubber in con-
struction materials, such as concrete, has received particular atten-
tion from researchers (Freakley and Payne 1978; Sienkiewicz et al.
2012; Medina et al. 2018). Both ductility and post-peak behavior
of concrete were found to be positively enhanced by the substitu-
tion of mineral aggregates with rubber particles (Eldin and Senouci
1993). However, rubber in large proportions (e.g., 100% sand
replacement) decreases the compressive strength (by up to 90%),
splitting tensile strength and elastic modulus (by up to 93%) (Naito
et al. 2014; Khatib and Bayomy 1999; Raffoul et al. 2017). Con-
sequently, RuC is not currently used in structural applications.
Recent research by the authors through the EU funded Anagennisi
project (Pilakoutas 2017) proved that FRP-confined rubberized
concrete (CRuC) with up to 60% total aggregate replacement is
able to withstand high levels of stress (up to 90 MPa) at large de-
formation levels (up to 6%) (Raffoul et al. 2017; Raffoul et al.
2016). Hence, CRuC has the potential to be used for the develop-
ment of flexible elements for a range of new structural applications,
including bridge bearings, integral structures, base isolation ele-
ments, coupling beams for shear walls, and zero moment connec-
tions. In many of these potential applications, the concrete elements
are subjected to high axial compressive stresses and large shear de-
formations. Although the compressive behavior of FRP-confined
conventional concrete has been extensively studied and compre-
hensive models exist (Lam and Teng 2003a; Alecci et al. 2014),
the behavior of RuC and CRuC has not been widely investigated.
Bompa et al. (2017) proposed a uniaxial constitutive analytical
model for RuC, whereas the compressive behavior of RuC and
CRuC was comprehensively examined by Raffoul et al. (2017).
Furthermore, limited studies on the shear behavior of this novel
material have been reported.

In the past century, the understanding of the structural per-
formance of concrete in shear has grown significantly around
the concept of the truss analogy. A significant step forward was
the development of softened concrete struts by Robinson and
Demorieux (1968) that reduced compressive strength in the pres-
ence of principal tensile stress. Various rational softened truss mod-
els have been proposed on the basis of the smeared-crack concept.
Vecchio and Collins (1986) proposed the compression field theory
(CFT) to predict the nonlinear behavior of reinforced concrete (RC)
elements by implementing softened stress-strain characteristics for
concrete in compression. This attempt was the first to quantify the
softening parameter as a function of the two principal strains. The
rotating-angle softened truss model (RA-STM) was generalized
by Hsu and his coworkers (Hsu 1988; Pang and Hsu 1995) for the
shear and torsion of RC members. This model considered both con-
crete compression softening and the tension stiffening effect. It as-
sumed that cracks will develop in a direction perpendicular to the
principal tensile stress and “rotate” to follow the changing principal
stress during loading. Because the crack angle assumed coincides
with the principal stress direction, neither the CFT nor the RA-STM
can adequately represent shear behavior because concrete shear re-
sistance cannot be developed along the principal direction. In con-
trast, the fixed-angle softened truss model (FA-STM) (Pang and
Hsu 1996; Zhu et al. 2001) assumes cracks to be oriented in the
direction of the applied principal compressive stresses and are fixed
at this angle thereafter. Because shear stresses can develop along
the crack direction, the FA-STM can account for the concrete con-
tribution in shear resistance. Although the predictions of these ra-
tional shear models are in good agreement with various types of
conventional RC structures subjected to shear or torsion, the appli-
cability of the softened truss theory to RuC and CRuC has not been
assessed.
The primary objective of this paper is to assess whether the
Fixed-Angle Softened Truss Model for RC proposed by Pang and
Hsu (1996) can be used in the case of RuC and CRuC and modify
them if necessary. This work is presented in four parts. The first
part describes the original FA-STM and introduces a modification
considering the properties of RuC and CRuC. The second part
discusses the required constitutive models for the implementation
of the modified FA-STM and how they are determined, including:
(1) concrete in compression; (2) concrete in tension; (3) concrete
in shear; and (4) FRP in tension. In the third part, a user defined
material subroutine (UMAT) is developed to enable the implemen-
tation of the proposed model in ABAQUS (2014). Finally, an ex-
perimental program on 18 prismatic samples is described and the
accuracy of the proposed model is assessed. This work will lead
to the development of numerical tools that will enable a deeper
understanding of the behavior of RuC and CRuC and will assist
in developing applications for highly deformable elements. The re-
sults presented in this study are part of the FP7 EU-funded project
Anagennisi, which aims to develop solutions to reuse all tire com-
ponents in high value innovative concrete applications (Pilakoutas
et al. 2015).
Application of FA-STM in RuC and CRuC
The FA-STM theory is based on six equations derived from two-
dimensional equilibrium and Mohr compatibility conditions. These
theoretical equations, along with constitutive material laws, can be
used in a numerical analysis to predict the performance of struc-
tures subjected to external loading (Pang and Hsu 1996). When
dealing with RC 2-D elements subjected to in-plane shear and nor-
mal stresses (τlt and σl, σt), the FA-STM defines three coordinate
systems (Fig. 1a): (a) l-t: the directions of longitudinal and trans-
verse steel bars, respectively; (b) 2-1: the directions of the principal
stresses of the RC element; (c) d-r coordinates: the directions of
principal stresses in the concrete alone. The governing equations
are derived by assuming that cracks in concrete are oriented at a
fixed angle α2, which is the angle between the 2-axis and the l-axis.
The angle between the d-axis and the l-axis is the rotating angle α,
which continues to rotate away from the fixed angle α2 as the load
increases. The angle between α2 and α is defined as the deviating
angle β ¼ α2 −α.
To apply FA-STM for unreinforced RuC and CRuC, the coor-
dinate systems have to be redefined (Fig. 1b) as follows: (1) l-t: the
directions of the longitudinal and transverse beam, respectively;
(2) 2-1: the directions of the principal stresses of RuC or CRuC
element; the angle between the 2-axis and l-axis is called the prin-
cipal angle θp; (3) n-m: the parallel and perpendicular directions
of the crack plane; fixed angle α2 is replaced by the angle of the
inclined strut; and, (4) the principal directions in concrete at any
stage are still represented by d-r coordinates, resulting in a rotating
angle θc. The deviating angle β is redesignated as the difference
between θf and θc. In RuC, the 2-1 coordinate coincides with the
d-r coordinate.

All tested specimens (cylinders and prisms) were manufactured
using rubberized concrete with rubber particles replacing 60% of
both fine and coarse mineral aggregates by volume. This “optimized”
mix was chosen from a detailed study on 40 mixes carried out by
Raffoul et al. (2016), which achieved the best balance between
strength retention and deformability. Table 1 provides the proper-

Experimental Program
Eighteen prismatic samples (500 × 100 × 100 mm) were tested to
assess the shear performance of CRuC and RuC. Fig. 17 indicates
the geometry and reinforcement details of the prisms and the
loading and support conditions. The central parts of the prisms
were cast with RuC (with a rubber content replacing 60% of the
aggregate by volume), whereas the sides were cast with regular
concrete. The prisms were reinforced with four basalt FRP bars
(∅4 mm) along the length of the prism. Basalt-FRP bars were
chosen for their low shear and axial stiffness to enable the develop-
ment of expected higher deformations within a shear span. Three
different shear span-to-depth ratios (a=d ¼ 0.5, 0.75, and 1) were
considered. For each ratio, tests were conducted on three uncon-
fined samples and three additional specimens confined with one
layer of carbon-FRP by using a wet lay-up technique. The overlap
length is 90 mm and is located on the top of the prism. Table 1
shows the material properties of the Basalt-FRP bars and carbon
fabric used in this research as obtained from direct tensile coupon
tests. The adopted test setup was the same as described in Fig. 13
and was used to obtain the shear stress-strain curve.
Figs. 18 and 19 show the average experimental shear stress-
strain and shear force-deflection plots for various a=d ratios of RuC
and CRuC, respectively. The shear stress τ was determined by nor-
malizing the shear force with respect to the cross-sectional area
at midspan. The shear strain γ was measured by two strain gauges
boned on the surface of the beam or the CFRP jacket and oriented
45° to the center line of the prisms. Given the pure shear stress
state at midspan, the directions of 45° are assumed to be the
principal directions of the strain. Therefore, the shear strain can be
determined by the two strain gauge readings, γ ¼ ε1p −ε2p. The
deflection was the average reading of LVDT 1 and 2 (Fig. 13).
The grey shaded area of the curve in Fig. 19 represents the range
of experimental results. This variability can be attributed to the
manufacturing quality of the FRP jackets and the inherent variabil-
ity of concrete.
A comparison of the results provided in Figs. 18 and 19 indi-
cates that both stress and strain at failure for confined specimens
are higher than for unconfined specimens, clearly indicating that
CFRP confinement enhances the load-carrying capacity and de-
formability. The hardening behavior of CRuC can be explained
by the fact that the confinement pressure provided by the CFRP
jacket maintains the integrity of the concrete and enhances its
ability to carry load. Although the load-deflection curves of
unconfined specimens are heavily influenced by their a=d ratio
(i.e., increasing a=d increases the moment in the flexural region),
the a=d ratio does not have a significant influence on the shear
stress-strain curves. The shear deformation of confined specimens
increases with increasing a=d ratio and results in higher ultimate
shear strain and deflection. Table 2 summarizes the average ex-

Verification of the Material Model
The finite element mesh used to perform the simulation of the
beams failing in shear is illustrated in Fig. 22. In the experiment,
the load was applied via a loading beam in displacement control
and was transferred to the specimen by two steel rollers (Fig. 17c).
In the numerical study, a displacement was applied at a reference
point, which was coupled with two nodal points on the beam
(Fig. 22). Four-node quadrilateral elements were used to model
the concrete, whereas two-node truss elements were used to model
the basalt bars. The perfect bond between the concrete and the
basalt bars was assumed. The total mesh consists of 130 plane
stress quadrilateral elements for concrete and 25 linear truss ele-
ments for basalt bars.
Figs. 18 and 19 compare the curves predicted by the proposed
model and the average experimental shear stress-strain and load-
deflection curves for RuC and CRuC, respectively. For the case
of RuC, the comparative assessment indicates that the numerical
modeling predicts reasonably well the ascending branch of the
stress-strain curves. However, the initial stiffnesses of the predicted
curves are generally stronger than that observed experimentally.
Furthermore, the numerical model fails to predict the load-
deflection behavior of the specimens (a=d ¼ 0.75 and 1.0) beyond
the load causing extensive flexural cracking (Fig. 18) given the
low flexural reinforcement provided. For the case of CRuC, the
comparative assessment between predicted and average experimen-
tal curves indicates good agreement.
Table 2 compares the critical material parameters obtained
from the experimental data and numerical modeling. For RuC,
the numerical values of the initial shear modulus (G0;RuC) are
higher than the experimental ones, whereas cracking shear stress
and shear strength (τcr;RuC and τp) are similar to the experimental
observations. For CRuC, the predicted G0;CRuC and G2 agree well
with the experimental values. The tendency of G2 to decrease with
increasing a=d is also demonstrated. The cracking shear stress
τcr;CRuC is higher than τcr;RuC because the FRP may control crack-
ing in the concrete. The transition between nonlinear and linear
behavior (τt; γt) in the numerical model occurs earlier than in the
experiment, which may be attributed to the fact that no gap be-
tween the jacket and concrete is allowed in the numerical model.
Thus, the jacket is mobilized earlier. The numerical ultimate
stress/strain values (τcc and γcu) are higher than the corresponding
experimental values, which may be attributed to the fact that the
effectiveness of FRP in confining concrete subjected to a combi-
nation of moment and shear is substantially different from the case
of concrete under compression only. Hence, this aspect requires
further investigation.
Conclusions
This paper presents the development and implementation of con-
stitutive material models for RuC and CRuC into nonlinear FEA
using a smeared, fixed crack approach. The material constitutive
laws were determined by combining the experimental methods
(uniaxial compression test, three-point bending test, and asymmet-
ric four-point bend test) with inverse FEA. The experimental work
used to calibrate and validate the model is also discussed in detail.
On the basis of the results presented, the following conclusions can
be drawn:
1. CRuC made with high rubber volumes (60% of aggregate repla-
cement) and confined with one layer of carbon fibers indicates
ductile and stable performance that allows the development
of high shear deformations (up to 40 times of RuC) at shear
strength values up to two times higher than for RuC. This in-
novative concrete can be used to develop highly deformable RC
components for structures.
2. By modifying the FA-STM, a new material model was devel-
oped that can be used to provide a reasonable characterization
of the shear behavior of RuC and CRuC. The abilities of the
model to portray the load-displacement relation and the shear
stress-strain relation are verified.
3. The model can be used to examine the shear performance of
highly deformable CRuC elements, such as bridge bearings,
shear wall coupling beams, or base isolation columns.
