A B S T R A C T  
In high-intensity seismic zones tunnel lining is generally utilized to provide efficient protection against tunnel 
collapse by withstanding pressure under strong earthquake events. However, this can be a challenging task in the 
case of fault-crossing tunnels, since tunnel lining follows the deformation of surrounding rock. To address this 
issue, the casing-shape tunnel lining system is proposed to reduce the constraint effects of surrounding rock on 
the lining structure by using an internal and external lining with a buffer layer employed in between. This 
research aims to investigate the rock-structure interaction and seismic response mechanism of casing-shape 
tunnels, both theoretically and experimentally, to facilitate the adoption of the proposed system. To develop a 
practical mechanical model for parametric study, the casing-shape tunnel lining is simplified as a Timoshenko 
composite beam. The theoretical analysis results indicate that the external lining and buffer layer can effectively 
reduce the earthquake-induced internal forces on the internal lining and there is an optimal length of casing- 
shape lining to balance cost-effectiveness with seismic resistance protection. To validate the proposed simpli­
fied model of casing-shape tunnel lining, a series of shaking table model tests are then performed on large-scale 
casing-shape lining models with earthquake-induced fault slippage under incremental input intensity excitations. 
The results demonstrate that: (i) the inclined angle of fault considerably affects the adopted length of casing- 
shape tunnel lining, (ii) the fault slippage shears the external lining directly leading to severe seismic damage 
at arch springing and invert, and (iii) the upper part of external lining and the whole internal lining generally 
exhibit a satisfying level of protection. This highlights the need to select appropriate cross-section shapes and 
structural materials for the construction of casing-shape tunnel lining systems. The outcomes of this study can 
provide theoretical foundation and experimental evidence for the seismic response mechanisms of casing-shape 
tunnel lining in practical applications.   
1. Introduction 
Although tunnel structures generally demonstrate good seismic 
performance, devastating tunnel lining damage may occur during strong 
earthquake events [1]. This is particularly prevalent in fault-crossing 
tunnels as the fault increases the exhibited deformations, and hence 
causes damage to the tunnel lining even if the transient displacement 
does not fully destroy the tunnel during a strong earthquake event [2–4]. 
This can be explained, since the occurrence of slippage and displacement 
along the fault is inevitable while the geological substance suffers 
earthquake effect, accompanied by intensive shear break. It is not al­
ways possible to avoid using tunnels in high-risk areas with existing 
secondary faults or miniature shattered fault zones, since straight lines 
or long sweeping curves are usually adopted in high-speed railways or 
highways, presenting severe security risks for the tunnel engineering 
constructed in high-intensity areas [5]. 
The seismic damage to tunnels can significantly affect their func­
tionality and the rescue operation after an earthquake event [6]. Espe­
cially traffic tunnels are the essential infrastructure for rescuing 
operations in disaster areas, and their failure not only hinders the post

During the 2008 Wenchuan Great earthquake in China, up to 110 tun­
nels exhibited earthquake damage, while most of the serious damage 
was observed in the areas where the condition of surrounding rock was 
poor and the displacement of shattered fault zone was very large [11]. 
Besides, the secondary fault influenced by the earthquake-induced fault 
was quite active, resulting in severe damage to a number of tunnels. 
As explained above, the damage to tunnels crossing fault or hard and 
soft rocks is more prominent due to excessive displacement as discussed 
above. In such cases, the interface between the fault and the surrounding 
rock plays a key role to ensure the seismic safety of long-line tunnels. 
Several researchers have conducted experimental studies aiming to 
investigate the effect of the inhomogeneity stratum on the dynamic re­
sponses of tunnels. For instance, Ref. [12] found that fault influences the 
seismic stability of underground caverns through a tendency in 
extending the plastic zones and increasing displacements as well as 
asymmetric stress distribution in rock medium. Ref. [13] investigated 
the location of shear zones and propagation of subsurface rupture traces 
through overlying sand. They concluded that the tunnel location, tunnel 
rigidity and soil relative density affect the rotation and displacement of 
the tunnel. In another relevant study, the non-linear responses of the 
tunnels within surface normal fault ground under obliquely incident P 
waves were investigated by Ref. [14]. The outcomes showed that the 
tunnels across the fault generally suffer the most from serious plastic 
damage. Similarly, Ref. [15] investigated the seismic stability of tunnel 
structures controlled by a large fault and showed that the dominant 
failure mode of the tunnel under earthquake excitations is generally due 
to shear slip at the intersection of the tunnel ceiling and fault. In another 
relevant study, Ref. [16] conducted shaking table model tests to validate 
the feasibility of adopting closed-cell aluminum foam layer and 
concluded that the layer weakens the dynamic response of the tunnel 
lining, while the shock absorption varied with the loading conditions. 
Ref. [17] investigated the complex mechanical properties of the fault 
using 3D dynamic finite element models. The results of their study 
showed that the most unstable state occurs when the fault strike is 
parallel to the longitudinal axis of the cavern and the intersection angle 
between the fault and the vertical axis in the cavern cross-section is 
approximately 40◦. In another study, Ref. [18] proposed two quantita­
tive damage indices for tunnels in fault fracture zones subjected to 
multiple strike-slip fault movements. Their numerical results indicated 
that the cross-sectional damage of the tunnels primarily concentrates in 
the vicinities of the fault planes, and the boundaries between the fault 
and the competent rock. Similarly, [19] showed that the internal forces 
and the residual earth pressures generally remain on the tunnel after an 
earthquake event, which may lead to accumulated damage to the tunnel 
system. Ref. [20] also investigated the seismic response of a segmental 
tunnel in the sand under near-fault and far-fault excitations and showed 
that near-fault motions can significantly aggravate the seismic response 
of both the sand ground and the segmental tunnel compared to far-field 
motions. In a more recent study, Ref. [21] introduced a method to 
simulate the fault slippage for the physical model of buried continuous 
pipelines crossing reverse faults, and demonstrated its efficiency to 
investigate the behavior of buried pipelines subjected to seismic 
hazards. 
Although several studies focused on the seismic dynamic responses 
of fault-crossing underground structures (e.g. Refs. [22,23], the 
earthquake-induced soil-structural interaction mechanisms between the 
slippage of fault and underground structures still remain an uncertain 
problem. Therefore, it is necessary to develop accurate analytical models 
to study the mechanical behavior of tunnel lining experiencing an 
earthquake-induced fault displacement [24]. To this end, conducting 
systematic shaking table tests to validate the adopted models and pro­
vide a better understanding of the seismic performance and damage 
mechanism of fault-crossing tunnel lining structure would be a key step 
[25,26]. 
This research aims to provide the necessary theoretical basis and the 
technical tools for seismic fortification of fault-crossing tunnel engi­
neering in high-intensity earthquake areas. In this paper, a casing-shape 
composite tunnel lining has been proposed, which is a new sort of tunnel 
lining structures composed of internal and external lining structures, 
overlapping with each other. Ordinarily, there is a buffer layer installed 
at this overlap and between the internal and external lining structure to 
avoid unnecessary collision during the seismic excitations. To bridge the 
above mentions research gaps, a fault-crossing casing-shape composite 
tunnel lining mechanical model is developed. The model is then used to 
investigate the effects of key design parameters including the foundation 
resistance coefficient for casing-shaped tunnel lining, the resistance 
coefficient for buffer layer, and the longitudinal adopted length. 
Furthermore, a set of shaking table tests are conducted on large-scale 
casing-shape lining models with earthquake-induced fault slippage 
under incremental input intensity excitations. The results are used to 
validate the developed mechanical model and assess the maximum ac­
celeration and strain values as well as the overall damage patterns of 
casing-shape tunnel linings under different earthquake intensity levels. 
2. Theoretical analyses and application 
2.1. Governing equation for dynamic response of casing-shape lining 
structure 
In the semi-infinite surrounding rock, tunnel lining structure can be 
deemed as a Timoshenko beam on the elastic foundation. Both of the P 
wave and S wave can lead to tension-compression and shearing de­
formations of lining structure during the seismic excitation, that is, the 
seismic motions cause complex responses of Timoshenko beam with 
constant axial forces. Thus, it is necessary to research the seismic 
response mechanisms of Timoshenko beam before investigating the 
seismic induced responses of tunnel lining along longitudinal direction. 
A Timoshenko beam is considered in the Cartesian coordinate system 
Oxy with the length L. The beam is subjected to a constant axial force FN 
and an excitation load w1(x, t) in the axial and transverse directions, 
respectively (see Fig. 1(a)). Since the P wave can cause tension and 
compression in surrounding rock along the longitudinal direction of 
tunnel lining during the seismic excitation, the P wave can apply the 
axial forces FN at the both ends of Timoshenko beam (i.e. tunnel lining) 
in equivalent. Thus, the seismic induced axial forces FN would promote 
the deformation of casing-shape composite tunnel lining in longitudinal 
direction in a narrow range, which cannot affect the mechanical model 
of casing-shape composite tunnel lining but make the mechanical model 
suitable for practical tunnel engineering. 
To avoid the unnecessary seismic induced collision between internal 
and external tunnel linings, it is necessary to install a buffer layer be­
tween internal and external tunnel linings. Thus, since the material of 
buffer layer was rubber sponge according to the similarity relations, it 
can only act as a spring between internal and external tunnel linings to 
generate the elastic stiffness coefficient of kb, but cannot provide support 
forces. Therefore, a buffer layer was assumed to be employed between 
the casing-shape lining of the tunnel, which constitutes a casing-shape 
composite tunnel lining structure (see Fig. 1(b)). The mechanical 
model of this composite lining structure is graphically illustrated in 
Fig. 1(c). Due to the high damping characteristics of the buffer layer, it 
can be simplified as a spring model with an elastic stiffness coefficient of 
kb. Both the internal and external lining can be regarded as Timoshenko 
beams using the same material. The external lining is connected to the 
surrounding rock and can be regarded as a Timoshenko beam on an 
elastic foundation. The elastic foundation resistance coefficient of the 
surrounding rock is kr.

2.3. Dynamic response verification of casing-shape lining 
Considering the actual construction process of tunnel engineering, it 
is assumed that the length of the casing-shape composite lining of a 
typical example tunnel is 3–12 m, the buffer layer is employed between 
the internal and external lining of the casing-shape lining. Based on the 
general design drawing of existing tunnel structure, the calculated area 
of the internal and external lining is 19.88 m2 and 25.06 m2, respec­
tively, and the rotational inertia of the internal and external lining is 
479.81 m4 and 562.35 m4, respectively. Static and dynamic parameters 
of surrounding rock, fault, lining and buffer layer for this example case 
are tabulated in Table 1. The corresponding numerical simulation model 
for validating this mechanical model are illustrated in Fig. 1 (d) and (e). 
As shown in Fig. 2(a), in general, the displacement response of the 
internal lining in the selected typical example is much smaller than that 
of the external lining. It can be seen that the analytical solution derived 
in this section compares very well with the results of finite element so­
lution, which indicates the validity of the proposed method. In addition, 
the bending moment response of the internal lining shown in Fig. 2(b) is 
much smaller than the dynamic response of the external lining, indi­
cating that the external lining and the buffer layer can effectively reduce 
the additional bending moment caused by seismic excitations. Further­
more, the displacement response of the casing-shape lining decreases 
gradually and converges to a stable value with the increase of the ratio k 
(which is the resistance coefficient ratio of the surrounding rock to the 
fault). Meanwhile, with the increase of k, the bending moment response 
of the casing-shape lining gradually becomes more significant. In this 
case, the earthquake-induced main additional bending moment is un­
dertaken by the external lining, while the bending moment response of 
the internal lining can be neglected, which demonstrates that the 
external lining and the buffer layer can provide effective protection for 
the internal lining. 
As shown in Fig. 3(a), in general, the displacement response of tunnel 
lining gradually increases with the increase of the length of casing-shape 
lining. Meanwhile, as shown in Fig. 3(b), the shear response of casing- 
shape lining is gradually becoming more significant with the increase 
of the length of casing-shape lining. This indicates that a major part of 
the earthquake-induced additional internal force is undertaken by the 
external lining and the buffer layer, thus reducing the response of the 
internal lining. To this end, there is an optimal value for the length of the 
casing-shape lining structure to maximize the seismic performance of 
the casing-shape lining structure considering the cost. 
As shown in Fig. 4(a), when the buffer layer vanishes, the elastic 
stiffness coefficient kb trends to 0 MPa. In general, the absolute values of 
the displacements of casing-shape lining are smaller when no buffer 
layer is set between the casing-shape lining, which indicates that the 
surrounding rock has stronger constraints on the external lining while 
the external lining also has stronger constraints on the internal lining. 
However, the absolute value of displacement of casing-shape lining with 
buffer layer is larger than that of the condition without buffer layer. It 
can be seen that in the case of setting buffer layer with kb = 0.05 MPa, 
the displacement response of the casing-shape lining reaches its 
maximum. With the gradual increase of kb, the absolute value of 
displacement of casing-shape lining all shows a decreasing trend. Since 
the foundation resistance coefficient of the surrounding rock is much 
larger than the elastic stiffness coefficient of the buffer layer, the abso­
lute value of the displacement of the internal lining is larger than that of 
the external lining. 
As shown in Fig. 4(b), when the buffer layer vanishes, the bending 
moment value is greater than the condition when a buffer layer is 
employed between the casing-shape tunnel lining. Under the protection 
of the external lining and buffer layer, the bending moment value of the 
internal lining is much smaller than that of the external lining. Addi­
tionally, the earthquake-induced bending moment is mainly transferred 
by the external lining, and hence the bending moment of the internal 
lining under the protection of the buffer layer is small and negligible. In 
addition, the increase of kb has little influence on the force of external 
lining, while the force of internal lining gradually increases with the 
increase of kb, but the cardinal number is still small compared with 
external lining. Therefore, the casing-shape tunnel lining can effectively 
reduce the internal force of the internal lining and improve the overall 
seismic performance of the tunnel lining by reducing its vibration 
amplitude.

3.3. Model preparation 
The internal dimension of the model container was 3.7 m×1.5 m ×
1.8 m (corresponding to length × width × height). There was a perspex 
sheet employed on the long side of the model container for observing the 
damage and structural response of the system through the experiment at 
any time. Additionally, both the long and short sides inside of the model 
container were installed with polystyrene membranes and polystyrene 
foams, respectively, to avoid the boundary effect from the model 
container as much as possible, whereas the mortar layer was filled on the 
bottom of the model container to ensure the high consistency of exci­
tation effect between the model container and the fabricated model 
system (shown in Fig. 6(a)). 
The materials used for fabricating the surrounding rock in this study 
included river sand (aggregate), engine oil (cement) and fly ash 
(accessory materials) using the mixture mass ratio of 31:12:57. This 
design was obtained via the repeating comparative selection of the 
orthogonal blend test, which was based on not only the surrounding rock 
class of the prototype but also on the similitude relationship used in this 
research. The obtained cohesive strength (c) and friction angle (φ) of the 
surrounding rock model was 3.01 kPa and 21◦respectively. The corre­
sponding dynamic characteristics of surrounding rock, faults and tunnel 
lining are listed in Table 3. Additionally, gravel with diameter ranging 
from 1.0 cm to 1.5 cm was utilized in the fault simulation according to 
the practical parameters of the fault obtained from the geological pro­
specting report of the selected prototype (shown in Fig. 6(b)). 
The mix ratio of the cement and medium sand was determined as 1:6, 
and the water-cement ratio was 1:1 based on the compressive strength 
test results of 70.7 mm3 cubic specimen and prisma specimens. The peak 
strain of the micro-concrete was determined as 300 με based on the 
compression tests as shown in Fig. 6(c). Formed steel wire mesh with 
1.06 mm diameter and 25.4 mm spacing in both longitudinal and 
transverse directions was employed to simulate steel bars. The length for 
the conventional tunnel model and internal lining model was 80 cm and 
120 cm, respectively. Fig. 6(d) shows the adopted test setup of the tunnel 
models. Herein, the casing-shape composite tunnel model consists of 
two casing-shape linings of different sizes, in which the internal diam­
eter of the external lining was 2 cm greater than the external diameter of 
the internal lining, while the spacing between them was filled with 
rubber plastic sponge to avoid occurring of adverse damage due to the 
direct collision between them during excitation. The epoxy resin glue 
was applied to connect the three tunnel lining models in the second 
group of the experiment.

of the shaking table tests (shown in Fig. 7(a) and (b)), while the con­

casing-shape composite lining and to study the damage mechanism for 
more efficient protection and strengthening of these systems in practical 
applications. 
The simulation of the fault was restrained by the single shaking table 
apparatus and model container. To provide an ideal condition for the 
simulation of the fault, nonconforming seismic excitations are required 
at the two sides of the fault for restoring the character of the fault.

3.5. Seismic excitation protocol 
Generally, the real earthquake motions can be recorded in three di­
rections as N–S, E-W, and U-D. Thus, considering the positions of pro­
totype tunnels and the earthquake epicentre, the E-W components of 
Wolong seismic motions was selected as the input motions for these 
series of shaking table model tests to investigate the seismic response 
mechanisms of casing-shape composite tunnel lining and compare the 
seismic damage phenomena in model tests as well as in the prototypes. 
The E-W component of the 2008 Wolong seismic motion was selected as 
the input seismic excitation in the test. The transverse exciting direction 
was horizontally perpendicular to the tunnel axis, which was deter­
mined based on the trend of the tunnel and its location in relation to the 
epicenter of the Wenchuan Great earthquake. Additionally, the peak 
acceleration and duration of the seismic motion were adjusted according 
to those of the actual record and the seismicity of the area of the pro­
totype [36]. The acceleration time history and its corresponding Fourier 
spectrum of the selected seismic motion are illustrated in Fig. 8(a) and 
(b), respectively. 
Regarding the earthquake excitation protocol, the peak ground 
acceleration (PGA) of the input seismic excitation was initially set as 0.2 
g, and was then gradually increased with equal 0.2 g steps until reached 
the PGA of 1.0 g. The deformation demands and damage mechanism of 
the tunnel model were measured under each PGA level. The exciting 
protocol was set not only based on the seismic precautionary intensity at 
the area of the prototype, but also to ensure the tunnel model can 
demonstrate clearly its damage patterns without integral collapse. 
Furthermore, the free field shaking table test was conducted before the 
experiment to obtain the acceleration responses of the surrounding rock 
inside of the model container, which was to validate the configuration 
rationality of the model container. 
4. Results of the analyses 
4.1. Validation of fault slippage device 
The performance of the slippage device was validated via a com­
parison between the displacement response of the surrounding rock at 
both sides of the fault. The results illustrated in Fig. 9s (a) and (b) show 
that there is an apparent displacement difference between the two sides 
of the fault regardless of the variance of the fault inclined angle. This 
phenomenon appeared more evident with the increase of the input peak 
ground acceleration, indicated using the statistic of peak relative 
displacement between the two sides of the fault in the two test groups 
(see Fig. 9 (c)). The data illustrated in Fig. 9(c) confirm that the fault 
slippage device could simulate the earthquake-induced fault slippage 
sufficiently since it achieved the practical displacement of the fault in 
the experiment. The vertical fault slippage and the fault slippage with 
the inclined angle of 60◦were equal to the practical displacement of 
2.78 m and 3.52 m, respectively when the input PGA is was 1.0 g. These 
results indicate that the displacement difference between the two lining 
segments, induced by the shearing effect of earthquake in practical 
tunnel engineering, could be well simulated by the adopted fault slip­
page device. For example, in the San Francisco earthquake 1906, the 
maximum horizontal displacement of the Wright tunnel crossing San 
Andreas Fault was 1.37 m, while the maximum horizontal displacement 
of a fault-crossing water-conveying tunnel was 3.0 m approximately in 
the Chi-Chi Earthquake 1999.

fault is directly undertaken by the external lining, the acceleration 
response of the external lining is greater than the conventional lining.

the fact that similar material is used for the internal and external lining, 
the external lining receives higher inertia forces, which also contributes 
to the foresaid test results. In contrast, the seismic response of the in­

tunnel structure is disconnected from the internal lining and the 
constraint force from the tunnel structure is also small due to the 
employment of the buffer layer. This can considerably reduce the input 
intensity excitation transferred to the internal lining as discussed before. 
Nevertheless, with the increasing PGA, the external lining is prone to a 
less intense seismic response after the input PGA is greater than 0.4 g, 
whereas the internal lining is more intense. 
The external lining aims to protect the internal lining, while the Ra 
results indicate that the external lining is more vulnerable to seismic 
effects. Therefore, the casing-shape composite lining can be constructed 
in the areas where the fault is less active, which can prolong the service 
life of the structure as much as possible, or chose to construct the 
interface of the internal and external lining at the fault to avoid the 
direct connection of the external lining and the fault. Moreover, the 
adopted length of the casing-shape composite lining can be determined 
depending on the inclined angle of the fault, while the material for the 
internal and external lining can be different to make the stiffness of the 
external lining greater than the internal lining to avoid the interaction 
damage under earthquake events.

owing to the mutual effect of the permanent strain and seismic strain. 
Therefore, the effect of the surrounding rock deformation during the 
earthquake on the lining structure is still considered even if the casing-

As illustrated in Fig. 12(a) and (b), the conventional lining reaches 
the peak strain of compressive cracking at the crown when the PGA of

lining is not cracked through the loading process. This indicates that the 
seismic performance of the casing-shape composite lining at the crown is 
better than the conventional lining. Besides, in general, the permanent 
strain values are always greater than the seismic strain, which indicates 
that the damage at the crown is mainly caused by the permanent strain 
due to the large deformation effect as discussed before. The seismic ef­

sufficiently reduced, which is due to the fact that the external lining is 
supported by the buffer layer and internal lining, whereas the internal 
lining is protected by the external lining from the surrounding rock. 
Furthermore, both the peak strain and permanent strain of the external 
lining under the inclined fault are quite large since the influence range of 
the inclined fault slippage is greater than the vertical fault. It can be 
noted that the internal lining strain highly coincides in the two groups of 
experiments (shown in Fig. 13(a)).

4.4. The damage phenomena of the casing-shape composite lining 
The final damage patterns of the casing-shape composite lining are 
illustrated in Figs. 14 and 15. The free end of the internal lining under 
the vertical fault is demonstrated in Fig. 14(a), there was only the invert 
appearing with penetrated longitudinal cracks, while the damage to the 
free end was lighter than the fault-crossing end (shown in Fig. 14(b)). 
There was no obvious damage to the internal lining under the strong 
earthquake-induced shearing effect, due to its smaller cross-section and 
larger stiffness along with the protection from the external lining. 
However, the damage to the invert of the internal lining was still 
noticeable. It should be noted that the invert cracking is mainly due to 
the weight from the upper structure of the tunnel lining as well as its 
large curvature radius. However, the slight cracking at the invert is 
negligible since the filling construction is generally adopted in the 
fabrication of the invert in practical engineering. Note that there was 
approximately no crack emerging at the arch springing of the internal 
lining, indicating the seismic protection is sufficiently achieved. These 
observations are consistent with the results of the theoretical analysis 
presented before. 
The final damage pattern of the external lining is demonstrated in 
Fig. 14(c). It can be seen that there was no apparent severe damage to 
the upper structure of the external lining since it was supported by both 
the buffer layer and internal lining. However, the cracking at the arch 
shoulders was distinct, which indicates the earthquake-induced sur­
rounding rock deformation determines the damage pattern of the lining 
structure. It is apparent that the damage to the lower structure of the 
external lining was more severe (shown in Fig. 14(d)), among which the 
arch springing was the most seriously suffered resulting in the 
outcropping of the steel bar, leading to the loss of its basic supporting 
effect. Furthermore, the strong earthquake separated the external lining 
from its upper structure and lower structure via the damage to the arch 
springing. Whereas the invert of the external lining was ruptured into 
plane formed due to the weight from the internal lining and upper 
structure adding with its large curvature radius. This indicates that the 
employment of steel bars is necessary since they can connect the upper 
structure and the lower structure of the external lining damaged by 
strong earthquake events. Moreover, the casing-shape composite lining 
structure, if the clearance between its internal and external lining is 
filled with buffer layer rather than soil, can sufficiently avoid the 
earthquake-induced pavement uplifting and preserve the upper struc­
ture of the lining structure in practical engineering.

5. Discussion 
The damage of strong earthquakes exceeding MW 6.0 to the under­
ground tunnel is devastating even though the underground structures 
generally possess some levels of seismic protection due to the constraint 
effects of surrounding rock [37,38]. Generally, tunnel lining is unable to 
yield the imperative deformation and resistance force to adequate the 
new balance state induced by the deformation and stress adjustment of 
the surrounding rock under strong earthquakes, which is the main 
reason for the earthquake damage to tunnel structures [39,40]. There­
fore, the dynamic action and evolution mechanism of rock-structural 
seismic interaction under strong earthquakes is the basis for lining 
structure design [41]. Additionally, the requirement for the integrity of 
the tunnel structure is strict since the earthquake-induced cumulative 
deformation of the surrounding rock can change the interaction between 
the lining structure and the surrounding rock. The deterioration and 
aging of the tunnel structure within its life cycle also affect its structural 
performance. As discussed before, the casing-shape lining structure of­
fers some seismic protections attributed to the interaction between the 
external lining and surrounding rock. The results of theoretical analysis 
and shaking table model tests in this study showed that the internal force 
and displacement demands of the internal lining were reduced 
compared to the conventional system, since the strong constraint of 
surrounding rock and earthquake-induced internal forces were mainly 
undertaken by the external lining. However, pressure on the internal 
lining increases with increasing the adopted length of the casing-shape 
lining structure, indicating that there is an optimal value for practical 
applications. It was also shown that the dynamic response of the internal 
lining under earthquake excitations can be sufficiently reduced by the 
buffer layer employed between the internal and external lining. 
The results of the shaking table model tests on selected casing-shape 
tunnel linings confirmed that the proposed theoretical analysis is cred­
ible, while it was shown that the principle for the adopted length of 
casing-shape tunnel lining depends on the inclined angle and influence 
range of the fault. Additionally, it is not favorable for applying the same 
material for the external and internal lining since they have different 
performances under the shearing effect and the constraint from the fault 
and surrounding rock correspondingly. The seismic performance of the 
casing-shape tunnel lining can be sufficiently improved under the 
displacement of the fault induced by strong earthquake events, when the 
stiffness of the external lining is greater than the internal lining. How­
ever, the casing-shape tunnel lining, within the tunnel systems and 
cross-sections used in this study, had an adverse effect on the protection 
of invert and arch springing of the external lining. These effects can be 
reduced by fortification of the external lining, changing the cross-section 
form of the tunnel to achieve a more reasonable force distribution at the 
lower structure, adopting different materials for the upper structure and 
the lower structure, and changing the geometry and material properties 
of the buffer layer, lastly, balancing the geometry and material param­
eters of fault and casing-shape tunnel lining. Investigating the effects of 
these parameters is a good area for further research. 
6. Summary and conclusions 
This research proposed a new seismic resistant casing-shape com­
posite tunnel lining system, which consists of the internal and external 
lining along with a buffer layer employed between them. The mechan­
ical model of this system was first established through theoretical ana­
lyses by considering the casing-shape tunnel lining as a Timoshenko 
composite beam on an elastic foundation. A series of shaking table tests 
were then conducted to validate the proposed theoretical approach and 
to provide a better understanding of the seismic response mechanism of 
the casing-shape composite lining and its ultimate damage patterns. The 
acceleration response and different strain responses of the tunnel models 
were analyzed for two groups of tests under earthquake-induced slip­
page considering vertical fault and the inclined fault subjected to in­
cremental input intensity excitations. Furthermore, a conventional 
tunnel lining was used as the benchmark to evaluate the efficiency of the 
casing-shape composite tunnel lining in improving the seismic perfor­
mance of the tunnel structure. The results of the theoretical analyses and 
shaking table tests were consistent with each other and verified the 
excellent structural performance of casing-shape composite tunnel lin­
ing in high-intensity areas. Some key findings are concluded as follows:  
(1) Based on the proposed theoretical analyses, the displacement 
response of the casing-shape lining decreases gradually (and 
converges to a constant value) with the increase of the resistance 
coefficient ratio k. The bending moment response of the internal 
lining is much smaller than the external lining since the addi­
tional earthquake-induced bending moment is mainly under­
taken by the external lining.  
(2) It was shown that the additional earthquake-induced internal 
force can be transferred to the external lining and the buffer layer 
to protect the internal lining. However, the displacement 
response and shear response of the casing-shape lining would 
gradually increase with the increase of the adopted length of the 
casing-shape lining. Thus, there is an optimal value for the length 
of the casing-shape lining structure to maximize the seismic 
performance considering the cost.  
(3) The seismic effect coefficient Ra was proposed to analyze the 
performance of the casing-shape composite lining under earth­
quake excitations using the results from the shaking table model 
tests. It was shown that the external lining is more vulnerable 
under seismic effect, while the seismic response intensity is 
orderly diminished from the external lining and conventional 
lining to the internal lining.  
(4) The results from the shaking table model tests indicated that, in 
general, the upper structure of the tunnel lining exhibits better 
seismic performance, among which the crown is the best pro­
tected position. However, the lower structure of the tunnel lining 
is quite vulnerable to strong earthquake excitation, where the 
arch springing and invert are generally the most severely 
damaged positions.  
(5) The ultimate damage of the lining structure is due to the mutual 
effect of the permanent strain and seismic strain, while the final 
damage pattern of the casing-shape lining is more distinct under 
the inclined fault condition. The damage pattern of the lower 
structure of the external lining can be significant, in which the 
arch springing and invert are the most damaged positions due to 
the weight of the internal lining and upper structure.
