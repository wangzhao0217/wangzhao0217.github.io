a b s t r a c t
This paper investigates the axial behaviour of square and circular concrete specimens conﬁned by exter-
nally bonded Fibre Reinforced Polymer (FRP) jackets. Axial compression tests were performed on 96 reg-
ular and rubberised concrete specimens. The parameters considered were rubber content (0% and 60%),
section shape (circular and square), FRP type (Carbon or Aramid) and the number of FRP layer (1, 2 and 3).
The behaviour of the specimens in both axial and lateral directions, as well as their dilation characteris-
tics, were investigated. The test results indicate that the conﬁnement effectiveness of Aramid-FRP is bet-
ter than that of Carbon-FRP. It is also shown that the detrimental effect of sharp corner on conﬁnement
effectiveness is less critical when using high rubber content as the element can develop large lateral
expansion at unprecedented axial deformation. Large axial deformations can enable the development
of highly ductile concrete elements. Existing design-oriented models fail to predict the behaviour of con-
ﬁned rubberised concrete with rectangular cross-section, indicating the need for developing more reﬁned
conﬁnement models.

1. Introduction
Enhancing the deformation capacity of reinforced concrete
structures can provide sufﬁcient resilience to sustain extreme
actions such as seismic ground motions and blast/impact loads.
The deformation characteristics of reinforced concrete elements
depend profoundly on the concrete compressive characteristics
[1]. The use of Fibre Reinforced Polymer (FRP) jackets to strengthen
and retroﬁt concrete specimens is increasingly practised world-
wide as it can enhance concrete compressive strength and
deformability. Extensive studies examined the behaviour of FRP-
conﬁned regular concrete (CRC) in both circular and rectangular
sections and evaluated the effectiveness of the conﬁnement [2-
7]. It is well known that for the same amount of conﬁnement, cir-
cular specimens perform better than rectangular specimens in
terms of ultimate stress and strain. This due to:
1) Conﬁning stress distribution; While a uniform conﬁnement
action develops in circular cross-section, in rectangular cross-
section, the conﬁnement action is non-uniform, with higher con-
ﬁning pressure at the corners and practically no conﬁning pressure
along the central part of the ﬂat sides. As a result, the conﬁnement
effectiveness is signiﬁcantly reduced in rectangular cross-section;
2) Stress in the conﬁning material; the average tensile rupture
strain of FRP in rectangular cross-section is much lower than that
of circular cross-section and is directly affected by the ratio of cor-
ner radius to the breadth of the cross-section. Tests show that a
very sharp corner (i.e. as the corner radius tends to zero) can even
severely reduce the conﬁnement [8-10] as the FRP jacket is sub-
jected to high stress concentrations at the corner. Rounding the

mines the ultimate state of FRP-conﬁned concrete, FRP composites
with high rupture strain (i.e. Aramid FRP) should be utilised to
develop highly deformable elements. However, the potential defor-

achieve an ultimate axial strain around 3.8%, regular concrete
cylinders (Ø150mm  305 mm) need to be conﬁned with 14 layers
of Glass FRP [9].
Recent research undertaken at The University of Shefﬁeld
within the EU funded Anagennisi project [11] assessed the feasibil-
ity of using rubber aggregates from end-of-life tyres to improve
concrete deformability and develop highly deformable elements.
Rubber is a highly durable material with good strength and
deformability, and can maintain its initial volume under stress.
This makes it an ideal candidate to replace mineral aggregates to
obtain highly deformable concrete. The use of rubber in concrete,
has received extensive attention from researchers [12-14]. It was
found that the deformability and post-peak behaviour of concrete
is positively enhanced by substituting mineral aggregates with
rubber particles [15-17]. However, it is also known that rubber at
large contents can dramatically decrease the strength of concrete
(by up to 90%). Consequently, rubberised concrete (RuC) has not
been used in structural applications.
In order to recover the lost strength, a number of investigations
have been conducted to use different types of lateral conﬁnement
to develop highly deformable conﬁned RuC (CRuC) elements, such
as cold-formed steel tubes [18] and Carbon FRP (CFRP) tubes
[14,19]. Although these conﬁnement methods led to an enhance-
ment in strength, the improvement in axial deformability was still
limited. Moreover, the use of steel tubes was proven to be less
effective for RuC than regular concrete due to local buckling, and
CFRP conﬁnement was was found to be less efﬁcient when com-
pared to other types of FRP, like aramid or glass [4,20]. Raffoul
et al. [4] found that RuC cylinders (with up to 60% total aggregates
replacement) conﬁned with four-layer Aramid FRP (AFRP) are able
to withstand high levels of stress (up to 90 MPa) at large levels of
deformation (axial strain up to 6%), while the ultimate stress and
axial strain of corresponding cylinders conﬁned with CFRP develop
only up to 60 MPa and 3.5% respectively. The test results also indi-
cated that for CRuC specimens, the conﬁnement provided by the
FRP jacket is activated earlier and with a more uniform FRP strain
distribution [21,22]. As a result, the conﬁnement effectiveness in
CRuC cylindrical specimens has been observed to be higher than
in CRC. Therefore, it is expected that the effectiveness of conﬁne-
ment in CRuC elements with rectangular cross-section would also
be enhanced. However, this has not been assessed experimentally.
The study aims to investigate experimentally the axial com-
pressive behaviour of FRP-conﬁned square and circular specimens
manufactured using regular and rubberised concrete. This paper
ﬁrst introduces the details of the comprehensive experimental pro-
gramme. Next, a discussion is carried out on the experimental
behaviour in terms of stress–strain and dilation. The inﬂuence of
various parameters on the conﬁnement effectiveness is then exam-
ined. Finally, the ability of selected design-oriented stress–strain
models to capture the behaviour of for rubberised concrete square
specimens is subsequently assessed.
2. Experimental program
Ninety-six samples, including 48 round (Ø100mm  200 mm)
and 48 square (100  100 mm in cross-section by 200 mm in
height) specimens, were manufactured using regular and rub-
berised concrete to assess their compressive characteristics. Three
replicates were tested for each of the examined parameters. The
main parameters investigated were the: 1) radius of the corner
(12 mm and 50 mm) (see Fig. 1); 2) rubber contents (qvr = 0 and
60%); and 3) type of FRP ﬁbre (Aramid or Carbon FRP) and number
of FRP layers (1, 2 and 3).
2.1. Rubber particles and mix designs
A reference regular concrete mix (qvr = 0) with the target 28-
day compressive strength of 60 MPa was modiﬁed to design the
rubberised concrete mix in this study. An optimised rubberised
concrete mix developed by Raffoul et al. [17] with 60% rubber
replacement by volume of mineral aggregates was used [17]. The
rubber particles were obtained from the recycling of end-of-life
tyres and were supplied in different size ranges (Fig. 2) [16]. The
speciﬁc gravities of mineral aggregates and rubber were measured
to be 2.65 and 0.8, respectively [17]. The quantity of rubber used in
the mixes was 330 kg/m3. The mix proportions used in this study
are summarised in Table 1.
2.2. Fibre reinforced polymer jacket
Before applying the FRP conﬁnement, the corners of the square
specimens were ground to ensure a smooth transition with the ﬂat
sides and to improve adherence with the FRP jacket. One, two or
three layers of AFRP or CFRP sheets were externally bonded to
the specimens using the wet lay-up technique. The unidirectional
fabrics were oriented in the hoop direction and overlapped by a
length of 100 mm to ensure the full transfer of the forces and pre-
vent premature debonding [4]. The mean mechanical properties of
the unidirectional FRP sheets were obtained using direct tensile
tests on 12 FRP coupons (see Table 2).
2.3. Test setup and instrumentation
All samples were subjected to axial compressive load using a
servo-controlled universal testing machine and tested in displace-
ment control at a rate of 0.2 mm/min up to failure [23]. Two alu-
minium rings (see Fig. 3) were used to mount the measurement
sensors (i.e. Laser Sensors (LS) and Linear Variable Differential
Transformers (LVDTs)). Three LS (an accuracy of 0.005 mm) located
radially at 120 with a 100 mm gauge length were used to measure
global axial displacement. Lateral deformations of the cylindrical
specimens were measured using three LVDTs, placed radially
around the specimens (120 apart, see Fig. 3 (b)), while lateral
expansions of the square specimens were measured using two
LVDTs (LVDT1 and LVDT3) located at the centre of two opposite
faces and another two LVDTs (LVDT2 and LVDT4) located at the
centre of two opposite corners (see Fig. 3 (d)). Local axial strains
(V1 and V2) and local lateral strains (H1, H2 and H3) of the cylin-
drical specimens were recorded using strain gauges placed at the
mid-height along the specimen circumference avoiding the overlap
region (see Fig. 3 (b)). Local axial strains (V1 and V2), local ﬂat side
lateral strains (H1, H2, H3 and H4) and local corner lateral strains
(CH1, CH2 and CH3) of the square specimens were measured using

Fig. 7 shows the average FRP hoop rupture strain (eh,rup) for both
CRC and CRuC specimens, which was always lower than the failure
tensile strain of the FRP coupons (efu) (i.e. efu = 2.06% and 0.9% for
aramid and carbon, respectively). This premature rupture failure
was also reported in many other studies [2,4,25-28] and can be
attributed to several factors such as non-homogeneous deforma-
tions of the cracked concrete that lead to stress concentrations in
the FRP, triaxial stress states of FRP during loading, geometrical
imperfections, and non-uniform supports in test setup, as well as
ﬁbre misalignment and overlap length. A detailed discussion can
be found in [27]. Nevertheless, these factors may interact with each
other and lead to a more complicated mechanism. It should be
mentioned that there is still no available rational model that can
be used to predict this phenomenon.
It can be seen that the eh,rup of CRuC specimens was higher than
that of CRC specimens. More speciﬁcally, CFRP CRC and CRuC cylin-
ders failed at around 75–80% and 80–90% of efu, respectively. AFRP
CRC and CRuC cylinders failed at around 70–80% and 85–90% of efu,
respectively. The higher eh,rup developed in CRuC was more pro-
nounced in square specimens, which reached up to 1.8 times
higher values than CRC specimens. This conﬁrms that replacing
mineral aggregates with rubber particles can lead to an enhance-
ment in FRP efﬁciency. It is worth noticing that the eh,rup of the
three-layer CRuC square specimens was nearly equivalent to the
value developed in cylinders. Furthermore, the eh,rup was found to
be independent of the number of FRP layers in circular specimens,
while in square specimens it was found to increase by increasing
the number of FRP layers.

strain distribution was unexpectedly observed in AFRP CRuC spec-
imens. In these specimens, the eh_C is signiﬁcantly lower than eh_F,
and slightly decreases with increasing the number of FRP layers.
This may be the result of considerable lateral expansion of the ﬂat
side of AFRP CRuC specimens (up to 6 mm), which creates opposite
curvature and reduces the relative bending strain at the corner. It is
worth noting that the value of maximum average strain recorded
along the ﬂat sides was close to the failure strain, efu, while the
strain values measured in the corners were only approximately
48% of efu.
In most existing stress–strain models, failure is controlled by eh,
rup, which is normally related to efu through a strain reduction
factor (kef). For CRC, the value of kef should be determined from
the hoop rupture strain at the round corner, rather than the strain
measured on the ﬂat side [29]. However, in CRuC, this will lead to a
signiﬁcant underestimation of strength and ultimate strain capac-
ity. Hence, a more rational way to calculate the value of kef is
needed.

4.1. Stress–strain behaviour

shown on the left (negative side). A detailed discussion of these
results is presented in the following sections.

while the corresponding CRuC column dissipates a total of
2.14  10-3 J. This greater energy requirement can be exploited,
for example, in seismic applications.
4.3. CFRP vs AFRP conﬁnement
Figs. 9 and 10 indicate that using the same number of FRP lay-
ers, CFRP specimens demonstrated a stiffer hardening response
than AFRP specimens. This can be attributed to the fact that the
stiffness of the CFRP jacket is about 1.5 times higher than that of
the AFRP jacket (see Table 3). However, due to the higher rupture
strain of aramid, the AFRP specimens developed higher ultimate
compressive stress and strain. As a result, the conﬁnement effec-
tiveness of AFRP was signiﬁcantly higher than that of CFRP.
In the case of CRC, the strength (fcc) of AFRP specimens was
slightly higher than that of corresponding CFRP specimens, while
much higher deformability in terms of axial strain (ecu) and area
strain (ea) was observed. In the case of CRuC, the CRuC specimens
conﬁned with AFRP were considerably stronger and substantially
more deformable than CFRP-conﬁned specimens, especially in
the case of square specimens. For example, the fcc/fco of 3-layer
AFRP CRuC square specimens was almost 2 times that of the corre-
sponding CFRP specimens. The conﬁnement effectiveness (ecu/eco)
of AFRP being superior to that of CFRP has also been reported in
the literature [19,25,26], but the improvement in both ecu/eco and
fcc/fco was only observed before in CRuC [4]. This may be attributed
to the very high axial deformability of CRuC, which results in large
axial loads being transferred to the FRP jacket. This can cause the
FRP jacket to lose its strength under the biaxial stress-state
(compression-tension). According to the Tsai–Wu failure criterion
for FRP [30], higher tensile strength can contribute to higher
strength under compression-tension stress state. Therefore, the
aramid is more suitable when developing high-strength high-
deformability elements for structural applications.
4.4. Circular vs square section
In the case of CRC, as expected, the conﬁnement effectiveness
(ecu/eco and fcc/fco) of FRP on square cross-section was less than
on circular cross-section (see Figs. 9 and 10). The increasing con-
ﬁnement stiffness produced a smaller increase in the strength
and deformability of square specimens than the corresponding
cylinders. The reduction of conﬁnement effectiveness in terms of
fcc/fco was also observed in CRuC specimens. However, unlike CRC
specimens, the deformability of CFRP CRuC was much less affected
by the section shape, and the AFRP CRuC specimens with a square
section even developed larger area strain than those with circular
section, hence exhibited better overall deformability. In Fig. 10,
the slope of the stress–strain curve of AFRP CRuC square specimens
was gradually increased at large deformation levels, which indi-
cates that the conﬁned shape was becoming more efﬁcient, result-
ing in higher conﬁnement effectiveness.

level (see Fig. 12 (e-h)).

The volumetric behaviour and secant dilation ratio of the tested
specimens is examined in this section to provide further insight
into the mechanical behaviour of regular and rubberised concrete
under passive FRP conﬁnement. Fig. 13 shows the average axial
stress (fcc)-volumetric strain (ev) curves of CRuC and CRC. The vol-

Figure 13 indicates that all FRP-conﬁned specimens exhibit vol-

stress fcr. While the critical stress (fcr) of CRC is equal to approxi-

remains in the range of 0.15–0.25 for regular concrete and 0.22–

tancy phenomenon appears to be affected by the level of conﬁning
pressure, thus the higher FRP conﬁnement levels result in a slower
volumetric expansion. On the contrary, CRuC specimens show an
entirely different behaviour, primarily characterised by volumetric
contraction until failure. This unique behaviour may be attributed
to the ‘‘ﬂuidity” of the rubber particles, which possibly ﬁll the voids
left by the crushed concrete. Similar behaviour was observed by
others [12]. The volumetric behaviour of AFRP CRuC square speci-
mens was characterised by an initial contraction followed by a
temporary expansion and eventually ended in contraction. This
double volumetric reversal has also been also reported in the liter-
ature [31,32], for regular concrete cylinders conﬁned by very large
amounts of FRP (e.g. 10 layers and 14 layers FRP). This observation
indicates that volumetric behaviour is also strongly inﬂuenced by
the conﬁnement stiffness (Kj). However, due to the limited test
data, the critical conﬁnement stiffness (Kj) that leads to zero vol-
ume change and could potentially contribute to establishing a uni-
ﬁed dilation model cannot be determined accurately.

6. Performance of Lam and Teng’s stress–strain model in
predicting the response of FRP-conﬁned rectangular concrete
specimens
Based on the results presented in the previous section, the Lam
and Teng’s model [3] offers the best prediction capacity among the
selected models. Thus, its ability to reproduce the stress–strain
responses of CRC and CRuC with rectangular cross-section will be
evaluated in this section.
The stress–strain curve of Lam and Teng’s model consists of two
smoothly connected parts: 1) a parabolic ascending part and a lin-
ear ascending part up to failure (ecu and fcc) [3]. The initial slope of
the parabolic part is determined by the modulus of elasticity of
unconﬁned concrete (Ec). The slope of the linear part (E2) is used
to reﬂect different FRP conﬁnement levels. The stress (ftr) is deﬁned
as the stress at which the linear hardening branch intercepts the
stress axis, and its value is considered independent from the con-
ﬁnement ratios and equal to fco for simplicity. The details of this
model can be found in [3].
As can be seen in Fig. 18 (a and b), the Lam and Teng’s model [3]
provides an excellent prediction for CFRP CRC, while much less
accuracy is observed for AFRP CRC. For CRuC, Fig. 19 (c and d)
shows that the model underestimates the ultimate stress and
strain, particularly in the case of AFRP CRuC. This can be explained
by the fact that the employed FRP rupture strain efﬁciency factors
(ke) cannot reﬂect the increasing rupture strain exhibited in CRuC.
Furthermore, the use of a constant value of ftr means that the
model cannot capture adequately the behaviour of the element
beyond the point at which the conﬁnement is activated. It should
be noted that the experimental results show that the inclusion of
rubber particles can enhance the lateral strain ability of concrete
under axial loading [4,52]. This enhancement is able to activate
the FRP conﬁnement at an early stage and provide a more substan-
tial conﬁning pressure in CRuC than in CRC. Therefore, the use of
critical stress (fcr) that is a function of the conﬁnement stiffness
as proposed by Raffoul et al. [21] and evidenced by the experimen-
tal results presented herein, appears to be more rational when
modelling CRuC. These results, highlight the need for the develop-
ment
of
reﬁned
conﬁnement
models
for
CRuC
rectangular
specimens.
7. Conclusion
This paper examined the axial behaviour of FRP-conﬁned con-
crete specimens with circular and square cross-sections. In total,
96 uniaxial compression tests were conducted to investigate the
effects of a range of parameters, including rubber contents, section
shape, FRP type and the number of FRP layers. The experimental
results were then used to assess the efﬁciency of existing conﬁne-
ment models. On the basis of the discussion presented above, the
following conclusions can be drawn:
 The use of rubber aggregates signiﬁcantly reduces concrete
strength, but this can be effectively recovered through FRP-
conﬁnement.
CRuC
specimens
exhibit
signiﬁcantly
higher
deformability (ultimate axial strains up to 5.7%) and absorb
more energy than CRC specimens.
 The ultimate conﬁnement condition of CRC square elements is
governed by the corner hoop rupture strain, while the ﬂat side
hoop rupture strain dominates the performance of CRuC square
elements.
 The conﬁnement effectiveness of CRuC is higher than that of
CRC in both circular and square sections. The effect of the corner
geometry on the effectiveness of conﬁnement in CRuC is less
severe than in CRC.
 The conﬁnement effectiveness of CFRP is lower than that of
AFRP. Furthermore, for the same FRP conﬁnement level, the
AFRP CRuC square sections show higher deformability (up to
19%) than the circular sections with an acceptable reduction
in strength (less than 20%).
 Unlike CRC, CRuC exhibits volumetric contraction throughout
the loading history. The secant dilation ratio of CRuC stabilises
at around 0.4, which makes this material suitable for the devel-
opment of high-strength highly-deformability elements under
shear.
 Existing FRP conﬁnement models for conventional concrete are
highly inaccurate in predicting the behaviour of CRuC, thus
indicating the need for further model development.
