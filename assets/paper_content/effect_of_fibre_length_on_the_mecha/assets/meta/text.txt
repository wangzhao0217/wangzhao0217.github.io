A B S T R A C T  
Despite significant amount of research on Recycled Tyre Steel Fibres (RTSF – extracted from end-of-life tyres) as 
reinforcement in concrete slab applications, the potential of RTSF is not fully understood mainly due to their 
variability in length (and aspect ratio). This paper investigates the effect of fibre length on the mechanical 
properties of Steel Fibre Reinforced Concrete (SFRC) using Recycled Tyre Cord Filaments (RTCF - extracted from 
unvulcanised rubber tyre belt off-cuts) which have very similar mechanical properties to RTSF. The flexural 
behaviour of SFRC prisms is evaluated using fixed lengths of RTCF (6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21 and 24 mm) at a dosage of 
30 kg/m3. Based on the flexural results, inverse Finite Element analysis (FEA) is used to determine the uniaxial 
tensile behaviour of the SFRC mixes. Mixes with fibres longer than 15 mm control macro-cracks well whilst 
residual flexural tensile strength fR increases almost linearly with fibre length, up to fibre lengths of 21 mm. 
Fibres with a length of 21 mm are the optimum for the mixes examined in this study in terms of post-cracking 
flexural behaviour and energy dissipation. The relative contribution of various fibre lengths (aspect ratios) in an 
RTSF mix is predicted well by using an equivalent energy approach. This work will help determine the efficiency 
of RTSF and speed up their adoption by industry.   
1. Introduction 
Approximately 1.7 billion tyres/annum reach the end of their useful 
lives worldwide [1,2]. Although these tyres can no longer be used for 
their initial purpose, their highly engineered constituents (rubber and 
steel wire) maintain their intrinsic mechanical properties (high strength, 
durability, elasticity, and damping) and could be reused in other com­
posites, such as concrete, to replace virgin materials. To extract the steel 
wire reinforcement from end-of-life tyres, tyre recycling plants usually 
employ reduction mechanical processes such as shredding and granu­
lation. These processes produce steel fibres of irregular geometrical 
shape, length and diameter and are often heavily contaminated with 
rubber particles (up to 20% by mass). As a result, these fibres are not 
suitable as reinforcement in concrete, since rubber particles can reduce 
concrete strength whilst fibres with large aspect ratios can cause fibre 
agglomeration (balling) and short fibres have insufficient bond to 
develop stress. Researchers at the University of Sheffield (USFD) [3–20] 
addressed this problem by controlling the amount of fibres with large 
aspect ratios and developed a method to produce clean, sorted and 
classified steel fibres by introducing additional processing techniques 
and employing a quality assurance system based on photogrammetry 
methods. The resulting Recycled Tyre Steel Fibres (RTSF) have minimal 
rubber contamination (less than 1% by mass) and are within a specific 
range of lengths (usually from 5 to 40 mm) and diameters (ranging from 
0.12 to 0.4 mm) [9,19,21,22]. Since 1999, USFD has conducted exten­
sive experimental work [4,5,7–9,13–20] to investigate the mechanical 
properties of Steel Fibre Reinforced Concrete (SFRC) using RTSF 
(Fig. 1a) and assess RTSF’s potential in structural applications. The re­
sults show that RTSF can partially or even fully replace Manufactured

In the majority of existing SFRC applications [30], a single type of 
MSF is used and the geometrical and mechanical properties of the fibres 
are selected to suit the specific application. The use of single-type MSF is 
effective in arresting or bridging cracks of specified widths, but the 
fracture process of the concrete matrix is multi-scale and progressive 
[30]. Since RTSF have a wide length distribution (and aspect ratio) and 
their nominal tensile strength is higher than that of typical MSF, they 
can potentially provide better crack control over a broader range of 
“micro” and “meso” crack widths. Nevertheless, previous studies [7,9, 
10,14,21,22,25,26] show that RTSF cannot provide better flexural per­
formance in SFRC than specially designed MSF (at the same dosage), in 
particular when “macro” cracks develop (>0.5 mm). As RTSF have 
variable length distribution, the inferior flexural performance at the 
macro level can be attributed to the inability of RTSF (with lengths in the 
range of 5–20 mm) to control macro cracks in concrete with aggregates 
of similar or larger diameter than the fibres. 
Fibres arising from the recycling of tyre steel cords (Recycled Tyre 
Steel Cords - RTSC, Fig. 1b [10]) have very similar mechanical/geo­
metrical characteristics to RTSF (Fig. 1a), but they are rubber-free, 
relatively straight in shape, and have the added advantage that they 
can be cut to specific lengths. These fibres are extracted from 
pre-vulcanised rubber belts (off-cuts from the tyre manufacturing pro­
cess) using a cryogenic process which freezes the belts and then extracts 
the reinforcement cords intact. A typical RTSC comprises 7–16 steel 
filaments which are twisted together to form the cord. This cord is then 
cut into predetermined lengths and the filaments (called Recycled Tyre 
Cord Filaments – RTCF, Fig. 1c) separate out. 
Whilst the effect of fibres in enhancing the toughness of concrete is 
well known, research on the effect of fibre length on the uniaxial tensile 
concrete strength is still inconclusive. Furlan and De Hanai [31] 
observed that the addition of 1–2% (by volume) of longer steel fibres, 
can increase the uniaxial tensile strength of concrete, whilst Meddah and 
Bencheikh [32] showed that small amounts of such steel fibres can only 
improve the post-cracking performance of concrete. Hence, it is critical 
to assess the contribution of each fibre length to the flexural behaviour 
of SFRC. 
This study aims to investigate the effect of fibre length on the me­
chanical properties of SFRC using RTCF. Since RTCF are made from the 
same material as RTSF, the outcome of this study can be used to improve 
RTSF quality and further understand the performance of RTSF rein­
forced concrete. To achieve this, 7 lengths of RTCF are selected with 
resulting aspect ratios ranging from 30 to 120, which fall within 
commonly used values for MSF [39]. 
This paper presents the experimental details, including RTCF prop­
erties, concrete mix design as well as test setup and procedures adopted 
to determine the compressive and flexural performance of SFRC with 
RTCF of different lengths. It then discusses the experimental results, 
together with the results of inverse analysis used to simulate the 
mechanical properties of SFRC. At the end, key findings and conclusions 
are summarised. 
The work presented in this study was part of the FP7 EU-funded 
project “Anagennisi” [40] which aimed to develop uses for all 
end-of-life tyre by-products (rubber, steel and polymer fibres) as rein­
forcement in concrete. 
2. Experimental details and results 
2.1. Fibre characterisation 
The RTCF used in this study were derived from tyre cord (RTSC) 
extracted from pre-vulcanised rubber tyre belts and cut to specific 
lengths (6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21 and 24 mm) with aspect ratios ranging from 
30 to 120. Each extracted cord (typically 0.5–2.0 m long) consisted of 11 
brass coated cold drawn steel filaments twisted together with a thin 
filament spirally wrapped around them to hold them together. The 
tensile strength of RTSC was found to be around 2600 MPa (based on an 
average of 30 samples) by performing direct tension tests [10]. The 
diameter was 0.20±0.01 mm. RTCF has very similar tensile strength to 
RTSF (2570 MPa [7]), indicating that the shredding process does not 
affect much the mechanical properties of the wire. 
2.2. Experimental programme and concrete mix design 
The concrete mix design was kept the same as the one used in pre­
vious studies by the authors [9,10] to enable direct comparison of the 
mechanical properties of different SFRC mixes. A dosage of 30 kg/m3 of 
fibres (fibre volume Vf = 0.38%) was selected for this parametric study 
as commonly used in structural applications, such as slabs-on-grade [9]. 
To assess the flexural and compressive properties of the SFRC mixes, 6 
prisms and 3 cubes were cast per mix (from the same batch of concrete), 
whilst 6 plain concrete prisms and 3 cubes (mix PC) were also manu­
factured and tested for control and comparison purposes. Table 1 shows 
the details of the experimental programme, including properties of the 
concrete mix (slump, density), modulus of elasticity derived from flex­
ural testing (Efm) and compressive testing (Ecm), as well as the average 
compressive strength (fcu) on the day of testing (65–70 days after cast­
ing). Each mix was named according to the length of the fibre used (for 
example, mix 12 refers to a mix reinforced with 12 mm long RTCF). The 
average density for each mix was determined from the prisms’ di­
mensions and their weight on the day of testing. Visual inspection of the 
fractured surfaces of the prisms, after testing, did not reveal any sig­
nificant void concentrations. 
2.2.1. Concrete mix design and casting procedure 
A ready-mix concrete with a target 28-day compressive strength, fcu, 
of 40 MPa was used in this study. The concrete mix design was as fol­
lows: 150 kg/m3 of cement (CEM I) and 150 kg/m3 of GGBS, 1097 kg/ 
m3 of coarse aggregates (4–20 mm) and 804 kg/m3 of fine gravel

The initial slump of the delivered ready mix concrete was 35 mm 
which is quite low for fibre dispersal. As the workability of concrete is 
expected to reduce further by the inclusion of fibres [21,42], additional 
water (10 l/m3 of concrete) and superplasticiser (4.5 l/m3 of concrete) 
were added to the concrete mix to bring the slump closer to 200 mm 
(considered optimum workability prior to the addition of steel fibres) 
and help avoid agglomeration and achieve a more uniform fibre 
dispersion The fibres were then dispersed into the concrete manually 
(the dispersal time varied from 5 to 8 min) in a medium-sized drum 
mixer with a capacity of 180 litres. After the addition of fibres, the final 
slump was brought back to ordinary workability levels (90 mm – final 
slump). Although the slump test is not the best indicator of workability 
for SFRC [43], it is still used by the flooring industry as a quality control 
method. Past studies [41,42] suggested that slump values of concrete 
could decrease with increasing fibre length (aspect ratio), however this 
study shows that there was no significant influence of fibre length on 
workability. This may be due to the small dosage used (30 kg/m3), as 
suggested by Centonze et al. [21] and Leone et al. [25]. 
According to BS EN 14651: 2005 [38], the prismatic moulds 
(550mmx150mmx150mm) were first filled in the centre and then the 
edges whilst being compacted by external vibration. No major fibre 
agglomeration issues were observed during casting. The specimens were 
covered with wet hessian and cured in the moulds for 48 h before 
demoulding. After demoulding, all specimens were covered again with 
wet hessian and plastic sheets to retain moisture for a period of 28 days, 
at a temperature of 22 ± 3 ◦C. After curing, all hessian and plastic sheets 
were removed and the specimens were left to dry. All specimens were 
tested at the age of 65–70 days. 
2.3. Compressive tests 
2.3.1. Testing procedure 
The concrete cubes (150 mm) were tested under uniaxial compres­
sive loading at a constant loading rate of 0.4 MPa/s, according to BS EN 
12390–3: 2019 [44]. The dimensions of each cube were measured prior 
to testing to obtain accurate estimates of strength. 
2.3.2. Compressive strength 
The SFRC average cube compressive strength, fcu, ranged from 43.2 
to 52.5 MPa, whilst the plain concrete average compressive strength was 
47 MPa (see Table 1). Overall, the use of different lengths of RTCF 
marginally affected the compressive strength concrete. Compared to 
plain concrete, mix 12 showed a small loss of strength (about 8%), whist 
the other mixes exhibited slightly higher compressive strength (up to 
12%). Longer fibres can delay the initiation of lateral microcracks and 
slow down their coalescence in macrocracks; the compaction of con­
crete, however, is more likely to be adversely affected by longer fibres, 
leading to strength degradation of concrete. A similar observation was 
made [42] for concrete reinforced with MSF (up to 120 kg/m3) with 
aspect ratios ranging from 45 to 80. Finally, the variability in concrete 
compressive strength of the SFRC mixes (see Table 1) was similar to that 
of plain concrete. Overall it can be concluded that the RTCF length does 
not significantly affect compressive strength. 
2.4. Flexural tests 
Uniaxial tensile tests on SFRC are difficult to perform and interpret 
[4,16,33,34], hence, flexural tests are usually conducted to characterise 
the nominal tensile characteristics of SFRC. Compared to other test 
methods, such as the 4-point un-notched prism [35], 3 point supported 
circular slab [36] and boundary supported square slab tests [37], the 
3-point flexural test on notched prisms [38] has the advantage of 
generating a predetermined crack plane, leading to better comparisons 
between SFRC mixes. Hence, for this study, the EN 14651: 2005 stan­
dard is adopted to quantify the tensile properties of the mixes with 
different lengths of RTCF. 
2.4.1. Testing arrangement and procedure 
According to BS EN 14651:2005, a notch (25 mm deep and 5 mm 
wide) was sawn at mid-span of each prism (550×150×150mm) one day 
before testing. All specimens were tested under a 3-point bending 
arrangement (Fig. 2), in a 300 kN universal electromechanical testing 
machine. To eliminate experimental measurement errors induced by 
support movements and the stiffness of the loading frame, an aluminium 
yoke [45] was used to measure deflections relative to the specimen 
supports, using Linear Variable Differential Transformers (LVDTs). A 
clip gauge (gauge length 12.5 mm) was mounted under the notch to 
monitor the Crack Mouth Opening Displacement (CMOD). Apart from 
deflection and CMOD, the applied load was also recorded during testing.

2.4.3. Stress-deflection curves 
The mid-span deflections of the prisms are determined from the 
average of the two vertical LVDTs positioned on either side of the 
specimen (§2.4.1). Fig. 3a shows the average (of six specimens) flexural 
stress-deflection curves across the full testing range (0–4 mm), whilst 
Fig. 3b zooms in on the first 0.15 mm of mid-span deflection to identify 
the behaviour of concrete during crack initiation. The curves for the 
plain concrete mix are indicated in solid black line, whilst the SFRC 
mixes are shown in dashed lines. As discussed in Section 2.4.4, the 
flexural curves for mixes PC, 6, 9 and 12, were extended to compare the 
energy dissipation up to 4 mm deflection (dotted lines). 
The plain mix showed the typical brittle behaviour of concrete, 
confirming the weakness of concrete in resisting tensile forces after 
cracking. All SFRC mixes generally exhibited a deflection softening 
behaviour, as RTCF debond, pull out or even break at different de­
flections (or crack widths), leading to progressive flexural deterioration. 
As expected, enhanced flexural toughness was obtained from mixes with 
longer RTCF (from 15 mm to 24 mm), highlighting the effectiveness of 
longer fibres in controlling macro-cracks, as also reported in [42] for 
MSF. However, mixes 21 and 24 showed very similar flexural behaviour: 
Although longer fibres have better bond and resist macro-crack devel­
opment, after a certain length, they may adversely affect the compaction 
of concrete, leading to degraded flexural performance. Mix 24 exhibited 
an increased tendency for the fibres to agglomerate, which means that 
fibre lengths above 21 mm need to be controlled to avoid balling issues 
at relatively high fibre dosages. 
Fig. 3b shows improvement in the peak flexural load for all SFRC 
mixes compared to plain concrete, which indicates that RTCF can be 
effective in “arresting” micro-cracks and delaying meso-crack initiation 
and propagation, as also reported in [9] for RTSF concrete prisms under 
fatigue. However, mixes with RTCF lengths of 6 mm and 9 mm still show 
a softening post peak behaviour, which means that their lengths are not 
sufficient to develop much of the fibre strength. Mixes with 12 mm (or 
above) RTCF show initial hardening behaviour, thus giving an indica­
tion that the fibres are developing significant stresses before slipping 
initiates.

2.4.5. LOP (fL) and residual flexural tensile strength (fR)

(LOP) and the residual flexural tensile strength parameters (fR) can be

CMOD of 0.05 mm, whilst four residual flexural tensile strength pa­
rameters (fR1, fR2, fR3 and fR4) are calculated from the post-cracking 
stage of the curves at 0.5, 1.5, 2.5 and 3.5 mm of CMOD, respectively.

A significant increase (15–40%) in fL is observed in the SFRC mixes over

delaying the fracture of the concrete. Overall, longer RTCF slightly 
enhance fL values (except for mixes 18 and 24). 
As LOP depends highly on the elastic properties of the concrete 
matrix, the slightly lower fL values for mixes 18 and 24 may be due to 
small fibre agglomerations or inconsistent compaction (which can leave 
air trapped around longer fibres). Up to 21 mm, fR values (fR1, fR2, fR3 
and fR4) increase with fibre length (Fig. 6), indicating that the maximum 
length for RTCF in concrete is about 21 mm with an aspect ratio of 105. 
Similar critical aspect ratios (i.e. 100) were reported by Hannant [41] 
and ACI 544.1R-96 [39] for MSF. 
Fig. 6 shows that fR values increase almost linearly with fibre length, 
l, (from 6 to 21 mm), highlighting the beneficial effect of fibre length 
(and aspect ratio) on the post-cracking performance of SFRC. This leads 
to strong correlations between fR1 and fR2, and between fR3 and fR4 
(Fig. 7), which means that fewer fracture parameters, e.g. fR1 and fR4, are 
sufficient to represent the post-cracking behaviour of SFRC at smaller (i.

In this study, this simplified trilinear stress-strain (σ-ε) model is

simulate successfully the mechanical properties of SFRC [11,48,57–60].

fibre-matrix bond, fibre orientation, or other material properties. Un­
derstanding the reasons behind this inconsistency and how it may affect 
the performance of SFRC mixes at shorter fibre lengths requires further 
investigation. 
2.4.7. Validation of proposed material model 
The proposed material model has been further examined using a 
larger mesh size (25 mm), as depicted in Fig. 14. It can be seen from  
Fig. 15 that the FE model using proposed σ-ε relations exhibits a 
reasonably good ability to predict the overall load-deflection responses, 
including the initial stiffness and the flexural strength with corre­
sponding mid-span deflection. However, the accuracy of the proposed 
material model in predicting post-failure behaviour was found to be 
lower, particularly for plain concrete. This can be attributed to the se­
lection of 65 mm as the corresponding characteristic length. It is rec­
ommended to choose a smaller value for better accuracy, although this 
was beyond the scope of the current study. 
2.5. Classification of SFRC mixes and slab-on-grade thickness analysis 
2.5.1. Classification of SFRC mixes 
To classify the post-cracking behaviour of FRC, Model Code 2010 
[33] introduces two parameters based on the characteristic residual 
flexural tensile strength parameters, fR1,c and fR3,c/fR1,c. The fR1,c rep­
resents the residual strength of a material at serviceability conditions, 
whilst the ratio fR3,c/fR1,c considers both serviceability and ultimate 
conditions. The strength class is denoted by a specific code name 
comprising a digit (based on the fR1,c value) and a letter (based on the 
fR3,c/fR1,c ratio), as given in Table 8. Model code 2010 [33] states that an 
SFRC mix can be used to substitute (entirely or partially) conventional 
reinforcement in concrete at ULS, if the following minimum perfor­
mance requirements are met: fR1,c/fL,c> 0.4 and fR3,c/fR1,c> 0.5 (where 
fL,c is the characteristic value of LOP).

values by assuming a t-distribution, and the number of specimens tested 
for each mix, according to RILEM TC 162-TDF [47].
