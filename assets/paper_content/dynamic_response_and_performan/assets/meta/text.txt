A B S T R A C T
Under mainshock-aftershock sequences, tunnel linings exhibit damage accumulation and delayed failure char­
acteristics. Based on large-scale shaking table tests and numerical simulations, this study systematically in­
vestigates the dynamic response and damage evolution of tunnel linings, with particular emphasis on the seismic 
mitigation mechanism of a buffer layer. Results indicate that shear-dominated initial damage first develops at the 
spandrel and arch springing during the mainshock, while subsequent aftershocks accelerate damage accumu­
lation and promote delayed crack coalescence. The buffer layer reduces peak acceleration, alleviates local strain 
concentration, and transforms the lining response from an inertia-dominated mode into a globally coordinated 
deformation mode, thereby suppressing secondary stress amplification during the aftershock stage. Energy-based 
analysis using Arias intensity further reveals a three-stage energy regulation behavior of the buffer layer. 
Parametric numerical analyses identify the optimal stiffness ratio and thickness ratio for enhancing the seismic 
performance and damage resistance of tunnel linings under mainshock-aftershock loading conditions.
1. Introduction
Southwestern China is strongly influenced by the Himalayan seismic 
belt and is characterized by intense tectonic activity. The extensive 
development of transportation infrastructure has rendered mountain 
tunnels increasingly vulnerable to severe safety hazards under strong 
seismic conditions (Yu et al. 2016; Yang et al., 2021; Xia et al., 2022; Xin 
et al., 2024a; Song et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025a). Although tunnels 
were historically regarded as relatively safe during earthquakes, major 
seismic events have demonstrated their non-negligible vulnerability. 
Strong earthquake events are often followed by frequent aftershocks, 
forming mainshock-aftershock (MS-AS) sequences that exert continuous 
impacts on engineering structures. For example, the 2008 Wenchuan 
earthquake (Mw 8.0) was followed by multiple aftershocks exceeding 
Mw 6.0 within 20 days; more than one hundred aftershocks with mag­
nitudes greater than Mw 6.0 were recorded within one month after the 
2011 Tohoku earthquake (Mw 9.0) in Japan; the 2022 Menyuan 
earthquake in Qinghai (Mw 6.9) consecutively triggered multiple 
moderate-to-strong aftershocks within a short period (Hashash et al., 
2001; Wang, 1993; Pitilakis and Tsinidis, 2014; Yu et al., 2016; Lei et al., 
2023; Li et al., 2025a; Sun et al., 2020; Yue et al., 2021; Luo et al., 2024; 
Yang et al., 2025b). These events demonstrate that tunnels in actual 
service conditions are commonly subjected to a continuous seismic 
process composed of both mainshocks and aftershocks. Nevertheless, 
most existing seismic analyses and experimental studies on tunnels as­
sume an undamaged structural state and consider only a single earth­
quake event Table 1.
Conventional seismic design approaches, such as Wang's (1993) free- 
field deformation method and guidelines following JTG 2232-2019 or 
Eurocode 8, largely assume a single earthquake event as the basis for 
analysis, emphasizing strength and deformation checks for one “design 
earthquake” while neglecting the scenario of sequential shaking. This 
indicates that the realistic evolution of structural performance under 
MS-AS sequences is not captured. In recent years, extensive studies have 
been conducted on dynamic responses and damage evolution of engi­
neering structures subjected to MS-AS sequences. Hatzigeorgiou and 
Beskos (2009) pioneered the analysis of structural demand under 
sequential earthquakes, demonstrating that inelastic displacement de­
mands can significantly increase when a mainshock is followed by af­
tershocks, especially for structures that have yielded in the first event.

produce substantial additional damage in steel moment-resisting 
frames, with significantly higher residual drifts and collapse ten­
dencies. Similar effects have been observed in eccentrically braced steel 
frames with energy-absorbing links and in tunnel-form concrete struc­
tures subjected to earthquake sequences (Mohsenian et al., 2021a, 
Mohsenian and Gharaei-Moghaddam 2021b). Goda and Taylor (2012)
investigated SDOF system responses to real mainshock-aftershock pairs, 
concluding that aftershocks typically cause incremental increases in 
ductility demand, though in some cases an aftershock near the struc­
ture's capacity can trigger collapse. In another study, Raghunandan et al. 
(2015) quantified how the collapse fragility of RC frame buildings in­
creases when accounting for aftershocks, showing that structures sur­
viving a mainshock in a damaged state have a considerably higher 
probability of collapse if subjected to even moderate aftershocks. Simi­
larly, Li and Ellingwood (2007) developed a methodology to update 
building fragility curves after a mainshock, showing that a structure's 
damage history can critically alter its future performance. These studies 
consistently demonstrate that MS-AS sequences represent a form of 
seismic loading characterized by continuous damage evolution. More 
recently, Lu et al. (2025) reported that ancient brick pagodas exhibited 
pronounced cumulative damage characteristics under MS-AS loading, 
with incremental damage strongly dependent on the post-mainshock 
damage state. Similarly, a study by Cui et al. (2024) revealed the full 
damage evolution process of subway stations under MS-AS sequences 
through centrifuge shaking table tests.
Nevertheless, despite these insights for buildings and bridges, the 
response of tunnels to sequential earthquakes remains largely unex­
plored. In general, tunnels damaged by a mainshock are more vulner­
able to aftershocks. Several numerical case studies have reported that 
aftershocks can accelerate tunnel lining deterioration even when their 
amplitudes are smaller than the mainshock (Sun et al., 2020; Singh et al., 
2018). However, systematic experimental or field investigations on MS- 
AS effects for tunnels are scarce. This represents a critical gap, since 
without understanding cumulative damage in tunnels, engineers cannot 
fully assess seismic performance or safety margins for tunnel infra­
structure under MS-AS sequences.
In this context, delaying damage accumulation in tunnel linings and 
regulating the degradation path of structural performance under MS-AS 
sequences are key issues in enhancing tunnel seismic performance. As a 
typical passive seismic mitigation measure, as illustrated in Fig. 1, the 
buffer layer has been widely applied in tunnel seismic protection and 
performance enhancement. The concept involves introducing a 
compressible or low-stiffness layer (e.g., polymer foam, rubber, or other 
material) between the tunnel lining and the surrounding ground to 
absorb shock. Early implementations involved expanded polystyrene 
(EPS) geofoam blocks, initially used to reduce seismic pressures behind 
retaining walls (Bathurst and Zarnani, 2007). When applied to tunnels, 
wrapping the lining with EPS or similar material can dissipate seismic 
energy and reduce transmitted forces.
Previous research has systematically evaluated the seismic mitiga­
tion performance of the buffer layer under single-earthquake loading 
through shaking table tests, centrifuge tests, and numerical simulations. 
Lu et al. (2024) verified the engineering applicability of buffer layer in 
large-diameter shield tunnels, confirming that isolation buffers can 
significantly reduce dynamic earth pressure and lining distortion. 
Similarly, Sun et al. (2024) study indicated that an EPS buffer layer with 
a thickness of 10–20 cm can achieve a favorable balance between 
seismic mitigation effectiveness and construction feasibility, with 
buffers thicker than approximately 20 cm leading to a resurgence of 
harmful deformation patterns. Xin et al. (2020) proposed a framework 
for designing tunnel buffer layers by examining the rock-buffer-lining 
interaction, and demonstrated that a circumferential buffer layer can 
interrupt force transfer from the ground to the lining, thereby reducing 
acceleration and pressure on the structure. They also identified optimal 
buffer properties, and reported that overly thick buffers may induce 
adverse effects like increased longitudinal accelerations. In a more 
recent study, Zhou et al. (2023) analyzed perforated rubber isolation 
layers around tunnels, demonstrating that a suitably perforated rubber 
layer can reduce lining stress more effectively than a solid layer of equal 
thickness by leveraging controlled flexibility.
In general, previous studies indicate that a properly designed buffer 
layer can redistribute seismic loads and interrupt direct stress transfer 
from the ground to the lining, while efficiently absorbing a portion of the 
seismic energy, thereby reducing acceleration and strain in the primary 
lining. However, Existing studies have primarily concentrated on the 
response characteristics of buffer layers under single earthquake events, 
whereas their working mechanisms, damage mitigation capacity, and 
contributions to structural performance under MS-AS sequences remain 
poorly understood. To date, almost no published research has investi­
gated the seismic performance of buffer-layered tunnels subjected to a 
mainshock followed by an aftershock. It therefore remains unclear 
whether the beneficial effects of a buffer layer, such as stress reduction 
and damage delay, persist or even become more pronounced when an 
aftershock occurs after the lining has already been damaged. On one 
hand, the buffer layer may continue to protect the lining by decoupling it 
from subsequent ground motion. Degradation of the buffer material or 
changes in system stiffness following the mainshock may also reduce its 
effectiveness.
To address the research gaps outlined above, this study conducts 
large–scale shaking table model tests to establish a tunnel-ground 
interaction model under MS–AS sequences. By comparatively 
analyzing the dynamic responses and damage evolution processes of 
tunnel linings with and without a buffer layer, and by introducing

2. Prototype case and seismic damage phenomenon
The Daliang Tunnel of the Lanzhou-Xinjiang High-Speed Railway is 
located in Menyuan County, Haibei Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture, 
Qinghai Province. The region is characterized by pronounced topo­
graphic relief and intense tectonic activity. The tunnel has a total length 
of 6,568 m, a maximum overburden depth of approximately 800 m, and 
adopts a double-track layout. The geological environment along the 
tunnel alignment is highly complex, with widespread development of 
tectonically deformed rocks, making it a typical tunnel project that 
crosses active faults in a high-altitude, earthquake prone region.
The tunnel passes through interbedded Middle Ordovician lime­
stone, sandstone, and slate, locally intercalated with fault breccia, fault 
gouge, and cataclastic rocks. The surrounding rock is predominantly 
classified as Grade V, characterized by intense weathering, extremely 
fractured rock mass structures, and poor overall stability. The tunnel 
crosses the Lenglongling Fault between chainages K1971 + 400 and 
K1971 + 680. The fault strikes N60◦-80◦W, dips southward with a dip 
angle of approximately 50◦-70◦, and has a fractured zone width of up to 
100 m (Huang et al., 2024; Xin et al., 2024b; Xin et al., 2024c). The 
hanging wall consists mainly of Ordovician crystalline limestone inter­
bedded with banded slate, while the fault is dominated by fault breccia, 
as shown in Fig. 2(a).
On January 8, 2022, an Mw 6.9 earthquake struck Menyuan County 
at a focal depth of 10 km. The seismogenic fault is a highly active Ho­
locene active fault, representing a high-risk zone for regional strong 
earthquakes. The epicenter was located 4.5 km from the Daliang Tunnel. 
The event was a typical left-lateral strike-slip earthquake (Liu et al.,

3. Shaking table testing program
3.1. Testing facilities
The shaking table test in this study was conducted using the seismic 
simulation shaking table system at the State Key Laboratory of Geo­
hazard Prevention and Geoenvironment Protection. The system is a 
large-scale three-dimensional shaking table with six degrees of freedom 
manufactured by Servotest. The table has a platform size of 4.0 m × 6.0 
m. The operating procedure of the shaking table is shown in Fig. 3(a).
The strike-slip active fault container is fabricated by welding Q345 
structural steel. The overall dimensions of the model container are 3.7 
m × 1.5 m × 1.9 m (length × width × height). To accurately simulate 
seismic responses and prevent the reflection of seismic energy at rigid 
boundaries, 20 cm thick foam plastic panels were installed on the inner 
transverse sidewall of the container prior to testing, as shown in Fig. 3
(b). Free-field tests by Feng et al. (2025a) were conducted to quantify 
potential boundary effects. The near–identical acceleration waveforms 
and less than 3 % amplitude difference between the centre and boundary 
monitoring points indicate that spurious wave reflections were effec­
tively suppressed.
3.2. Scaling relationships
Geometric dimension, density, and elastic modulus were selected as 
the three fundamental control parameters. The density similarity ratio 
was determined as 1:1.3, and the elastic modulus similarity ratio was set 
to 1:39. In the experiment, because the gravitational conditions and 
strain characteristics are consistent with those of the prototype tunnel, 
the scaling ratios of gravitational acceleration and strain were both 
taken as 1. The remaining physical quantities were derived based on 
Buckingham’s π theorem and the MLT dimensional analysis criterion, as 
summarized in Table 3.
Based on the model test results reported by Xin et al. (2018), fly ash, 
river sand, and engine oil were selected to prepare the surrounding rock 
similarity material. Using orthogonal test results, the mass ratios of fly 
ash, river sand, and engine oil were determined to be 67:25:8 for the 
surrounding rock material and 65:22:13 for the fault material. For the 
tunnel lining, a mixture composed of water, gypsum, diatomite, quartz 
sand, and barite powder with a mass ratio of 10:6.5:1:1.5:1 was adopted. 
The corresponding mechanical parameters of the prototype and the 
scaled model are listed in Table 4.
The fabrication process of the tunnel lining model is shown in Fig. 4
(a). The tunnel-surrounding rock system was constructed using an in- 
situ casting method. The similarity materials were placed and com­
pacted layer by layer following the principle of volume control, with 
layered sampling conducted during construction, as illustrated in Fig. 4
(b).
In the experiment, a rubber-plastic sponge with a thickness of 1 cm 
was used to simulate the buffer layer. The buffer layer had an elastic 
modulus of 1.38 MPa, a density of 0.06 g/cm3, and a Poisson’s ratio of 
0.3. Fig. 4(c) illustrates the details of the buffer layer used in the tunnel 
lining model.
3.3. Experimental plan
The sensors and auxiliary equipment used in this experiment are 
shown in Fig. 5(a). Strain gauges, accelerometers, earth pressure cells, 
and laser displacement transducers were employed to monitor the 
circumferential strain of the tunnel lining, acceleration responses, 
lining-surrounding rock interaction, and ground surface settlement, 
respectively. Two action cameras and two tablet display units were used 
to monitor the deformation and cracking of the tunnel lining during the 
experiment.
The tunnel linings used in the experiment were classified into three 
types: conventional linings, plain gypsum lining (without reinforcing 
mesh), and buffer-layer lining (externally wrapped with a buffer layer). 
Segment B corresponds to the lining with a buffer layer, whereas 
Segment G represents the plain gypsum lining, and the remaining seg­
ments are conventional linings (Fig. 5(b)). The lining length at both 
tunnel portals was 50 cm, with 10 cm embedded into the foam bound­
ary, while all other lining segments had a length of 40 cm. In total, 31 
accelerometers, 24 earth pressure cells, 4 laser displacement trans­
ducers, and 72 strain gauges were used in this experiment. Eight 
monitoring sections (monitoring sections 0-7) were selected along the 
tunnel. At each monitoring section, earth pressure cells were installed on 
the outer side of the lining, while accelerometers and strain gauges were 
mounted on both the inner and outer surfaces. The layout of the sensors 
is shown in Fig. 5(b).
3.4. Earthquake motions
The repetitive superposition method was adopted to construct the 
MS‑AS loading sequence for the shaking table tests, owing to its inherent 
compatibility with the stepwise loading strategy involving progressively 
increasing peak ground acceleration. By scaling the recorded mainshock 
wave with different amplitude coefficients, this method preserves the 
waveform, frequency content, and duration between the mainshock and 
aftershocks, while enabling independent control of the aftershock in­
tensity. Based on the ground motion records during the Menyuan 
earthquake, an MS-AS sequence was synthesized using the repetitive 
superposition method (Hatzigeorgiou et al., 2009; Joyner and Boore 
1982). The acceleration time history and Fourier spectrum of the re­
cord are shown in Fig. 6(a) and Fig. 6(b), respectively. The synthesized 
sequence was then used as the input excitation for the shaking-table 
tests. The peak acceleration of the mainshock in the shaking table 
tests was set to 0.5 g to be consistent with the peak ground acceleration 
recorded during the Menyuan earthquake. The mainshock was applied 
through stepwise and continuous loading with peak accelerations of 0.1 
g, 0.2 g, 0.3 g, 0.4 g, and 0.5 g, as shown in Fig. 6(c). After the mainshock

buffer layer significantly delays the damage development process of the

are delayed compared with those in lining segments without buffer 
layers, resulting in an overall reduction in damage severity. However, 
the buffer-layer lining segment still exhibits crack initiation locations 
and development paths similar to those of conventional linings, sug­

characteristics or the dominant failure zones of the lining. Instead, it 
primarily affects the rate and temporal sequence of damage progression.

will continue to propagate along existing paths, and cracks near the 
spandrel side of the crown may eventually become through-going, 
causing the buffer-layer lining segment to exhibit failure patterns 
similar to those of Segment G under extreme conditions.
4.2. Lining strain response
During an earthquake, the propagation of seismic waves induces 
vibrations in both the tunnel structure and the surrounding rock mass. 
When the induced strain exceeds the strain capacity of the lining ma­
terial, cracking and structural damage occur. Strain measurements of the 
tunnel lining provide direct insight into its stress state and damage 
evolution. Under seismic excitation, the strain of the tunnel lining 
dominated by tensile stress exhibits a clear stage-wise characteristic, 
which can be classified into three typical stages, as illustrated in Fig. 8. 
In the initial stage, the external dynamic loading is relatively weak, and 
the lining responds elastically. The strain increases approximately lin­
early with increasing seismic intensity and almost fully recovers upon 
unloading. As the seismic excitation intensifies, the lining gradually 
enters the damage accumulation stage. In this stage, only partial strain 
recovery is observed after unloading, and residual strain accumulates 
progressively. When the seismic action further increases and approaches 
the tensile strength limit of the material, the lining enters the failure 
stage, during which cracks rapidly propagate and coalesce, resulting in 
pronounced local deformation. These three stages demonstrate that 
seismic damage of the tunnel lining is not instantaneous but rather a 
progressive dynamic failure process evolving from elastic response to 
damage accumulation and finally to macroscopic cracking.
In Case 1 (MS-PGA = 0.1 g), Fig. 9 shows the strain time histories at 
the outer lining locations, including the crown, spandrel, sidewall, and 
invert. At this low PGA level, both the buffer-layer lining and the plain 
gypsum lining exhibit symmetric and similar peak strain responses at the 
spandrel and arch springing, indicating that the lining remains in an 
overall elastic deformation state. Due to the shear action of seismic 
waves, strain responses along the spandrel-arch springing direction are 
significantly larger than those at the invert, crown, and sidewall. 
Moreover, the strain time histories along this direction vary synchro­
nously with the input seismic waveform. As shown in Fig. 9(a), for 
monitoring section 1 (abbreviated as Section 1), the peak strains at the 
arch springing reach −26με (left) and 21.5με (right), which are sub­
stantially larger than those at the spandrel (7.0με on the left and −9.3με 
on the right). This indicates that greater deformation occurs at the arch 
springing during dynamic loading, which is consistent with previous 
experimental findings and observed seismic damage patterns in real 
tunnels. As shown in Fig. 9(b), the peak strains at the spandrel of Section 
1 (14.6με on the left and 17.2με on the right) are significantly larger than 
those of Section 6, whereas the peak strains at the arch springing of 
Section 1 (14.6με on the left and −13.4με on the right) are smaller than 
those of Section 6. This suggests that the buffer layer redistributes the 
interaction forces transmitted from the surrounding rock more uni­
formly over the entire lining cross-section, thereby improving the stress 
state and enhancing the structural enhancing the structural performance

4.4. Arias intensity
Strain and acceleration analyses capture the local damage evolution 
and global dynamic response of tunnel linings, respectively. However, 
under MS-AS sequences, structural damage is not governed by instan­
taneous peak responses, but by the cumulative seismic energy input and 
its temporal distribution. Therefore, peak-based indices alone cannot 
adequately represent damage accumulation and delayed failure. From 
an energy perspective, seismic damage results from the transmission, 
dissipation, and accumulation of input energy within the lining-rock 
system. Arias intensity, which integrates the squared acceleration over 
time, concisely measures cumulative seismic energy, accounting 
simultaneously for ground-motion amplitude and duration (Xin et al., 
2024b; Li et al., 2025b). The Arias intensity is defined as:

Accordingly, Arias intensity is introduced to compare the energy 
input and its evolution in linings with and without buffer layers, 
enabling the stage-dependent energy regulation mechanism and the 
seismic performance enhancement effect of the buffer layer to be clearly 
identified under MS-AS sequences. As shown in Fig. 14, the Arias in­
tensity time histories of tunnel linings under different loading cases are 
presented together with the corresponding acceleration time histories, 
where the effective duration corresponding to the accumulation of 5 % 
to 95 % of the total energy is highlighted in cyan. Overall, during both 
the mainshock and aftershock stages, the Arias intensity of the lining 
equipped with a buffer layer (A1-1) is significantly lower than that of the 
plain gypsum lining, indicating that the buffer layer effectively reduces 
the overall seismic energy input to the lining system. In terms of energy 
growth rate, the Arias intensity curve of A1-1 exhibits a noticeably 
smaller slope, indicating a more gradual energy input process. Mean­
while, the curve is smoother and more continuous, suggesting that the 
buffer layer attenuates pulse-type energy components in the seismic 
motion, reduces abrupt energy jumps during energy input, and thereby 
optimizes the energy input path of the lining system.
With a further increase in the seismic energy level of the input mo­
tion (Case 5), as shown in Fig. 14(e), although the total energy input to 
A1-1 remains lower than that of the case without a buffer layer, the 
growth slope of its Arias intensity curve during the 0–18 s interval 
gradually approaches that of the reference case. This indicates that 
under high-energy input conditions, the energy-filtering efficiency of the 
buffer layer tends to become saturated. The primary differences between 
the two cases are concentrated in the subsequent low- to moderate- 
amplitude energy input stage after 18 s, indicating that the buffer 
layer still maintains a sustained regulatory capacity for later-stage en­
ergy input. Fig. 14(f) shows that under aftershock loading (Case 6), the 
buffer layer effectively reduced the cumulative energy absorption of the 
lining and regulated the energy input profile, even following mainshock- 
induced damage. These results highlight the buffer layer’s robust energy 
dissipation capability and its efficacy in controlling cumulative damage 
under MS-AS sequences.
To quantify the net regulating effect of the buffer layer on the energy 
input to the lining throughout the seismic process, the Arias intensity 
time histories from two locations were compared: point A1-1 and point 
A6-1. The difference between these time histories was calculated, 
yielding the evolution of the Arias intensity difference, as shown in 
Fig. 15(a). Under identical seismic input conditions, this difference 
curve directly reflects the cumulative energy reduction achieved by the 
buffer layer relative to the conventional lining and its temporal evolu­
tion, thereby effectively eliminating the influence of ground-motion 
amplitude and duration differences on the energy metric and high­
lighting the intrinsic energy regulation effect of the buffer layer.
The evolution of the Arias intensity difference curve reveals distinct 
stage-wise characteristics in the energy regulation performed by the 
buffer layer. During the initial phase of seismic excitation, the difference 
increases gradually, corresponding to a period of weak overall system 
response, in which the buffer layer is not yet fully mobilized. As the 
ground motion intensifies, the curve rises sharply, indicating an effec­
tive energy dissipation stage. During this stage, the buffer layer sub­
stantially reduces the seismic energy transmitted to the lining, acting as 
the primary medium for filtering and dissipating input energy. 
Following the main energy input, the difference curve gradually stabi­
lizes, entering a performance convergence stage where the energy-input 
gap no longer widens significantly. This reflects that the regulatory ef­
ficiency of the buffer layer tends to stabilize under the prevailing energy 
level and structural state.
Based on the stage-dependent evolution characteristics of the Arias 
intensity difference time history, the energy regulation mechanism of 
the buffer layer during seismic excitation can be further abstracted into a 
three-stage model consisting of energy initiation, effective energy 
dissipation, and performance convergence, as summarized by the 
mechanistic schematic in Fig. 15(b). This staged behavior indicates that 
the buffer layer does not operate with constant efficiency throughout the 
entire seismic process; instead, its energy regulation capability dynam­
ically evolves with both the seismic energy input level and the structural 
response state.

To further elucidate the influence of buffer layer parameters on the 
seismic response of tunnel linings and to overcome the limited gen­
eralisability of conclusions drawn from a single experimental configu­
ration, this section presents a systematic parametric analysis of buffer 
layer stiffness and thickness based on the validated numerical model. 
The stiffness ratio EB/ET is defined as the ratio of the elastic modulus of 
the buffer layer to that of the tunnel lining. Eleven discrete values 
ranging from 0.1 % to 100 % (0.1 %, 1.0 %, 2.5 %, 5.0 %, 6.5 %, 10 %, 
20 %, 40 %, 60 %, 80 %, 100 %) are selected for numerical simulation. 
Fig. 19(a) shows the variation of lining damage area with the stiffness 
ratio.
The results demonstrate a strongly nonlinear relationship between 
damage area and stiffness ratio. When EB/ET = 0.1 %, the damage area 
reaches 16.87 %, which is slightly higher than that of the benchmark 
case without a buffer layer. This is because an excessively low buffer 
layer stiffness fails to provide effective lateral confinement, leading to 
excessive relative displacement between the lining and the surrounding 
rock. The energy of the seismically induced shear waves cannot be 
adequately dissipated through the interface, and the structure exhibits a 
near free–vibration state, which paradoxically intensifies local stress 
concentrations.
As the stiffness ratio increases into the range of 1.0 %-6.5 %, the 
damage area decreases significantly. When EB/ET reaches 10 % and 
further increases to 20 %, the damage area drops sharply and stabilises 
at a low plateau of approximately 5 %-6 %, which persists up to EB/ET =
100 %. This indicates that with an appropriate impedance matching 
characteristic, the buffer layer enables effective reflection and trans­
mission–induced attenuation of seismic waves at the lining–rock inter­
face, while allowing compatible relative deformation between the lining 
and the surrounding rock. Consequently, the typical ovaling deforma­
tion and transverse squeezing modes are substantially suppressed, input 
energy is largely dissipated, and the risk of concrete cracking is reduced. 
Thus, the seismic mitigation efficiency of the buffer layer does not follow 
a “softer is better” rule; rather, there exists a clearly defined optimal 
stiffness range (in this study, EB/ET ≥10 %).
It is worth noting that once the stiffness ratio enters the above stable 
range, the residual damage is almost entirely concentrated in the 
arch–foot region. This distribution characteristic is closely related to the 
geometry of the horseshoe-shaped tri-centric circular tunnel: the crown 
and sidewalls are connected by three arc segments with different centres 
and radii, resulting in a pronounced curvature jump and geometric 
discontinuity at the arch springing, where the structural stiffness 
gradient changes dramatically. Under earthquake–induced transverse 
squeezing deformation, the arch springing – being the critical transition 
zone where the curvature of the arch turns towards the straight segment 
of the sidewall – experiences highly concentrated bending moments and 
shear stresses, with local peak stresses significantly higher than in other 
regions. Therefore, although the flexible isolation of the buffer layer can 
improve the overall tunnel-rock dynamic interaction, its ability to 
directly mitigate the inherent stress concentration at the arch springing 
is relatively limited. This finding suggests that in practical engineering, 
the buffer layer should be used in combination with local reinforcement 
measures at the arch springing to achieve more comprehensive seismic 
protection.

and 7.2 %, respectively. This evolutionary pattern indicates that the

rock, promoting a transition of the lining from an “inertia–dominated

In terms of acceleration response, the mitigation effect of the buffer 
layer exhibits a clear intensity dependence: the reduction is insignificant 
under low–amplitude mainshock excitation, but under strong main­
shock the overall peak acceleration is reduced by more than 30 %, with a 
reduction of up to 38 % at the invert. This indicates that the buffer layer 
is not fully mobilised at low energy input levels; its primary contribution 
lies not in directly reducing inertial forces, but in modifying the force 
transmission path at the rock–lining interface.
Energy analysis further reveals the essential role of the buffer layer 
during the mainshock. The Arias intensity curve of the buffered lining is 
consistently lower than that of the unbuffered lining, and the energy 
accumulation is gentler and more continuous, indicating that the buffer 
layer can filter out impulsive energy components and prevent concen­
trated energy input over a short period. The stage–wise characteristics of 
the difference curve demonstrate that the regulation efficiency of the 
buffer layer is not constant but evolves dynamically with the seismic 
energy level and the structural response state.
The core function of the buffer layer during the mainshock stage is to 
delay the initiation and propagation of initial damage. By altering the 
dynamic force transmission path through a flexible interface, it sup­
presses shear strain concentration, enabling the lining to maintain a 
lower level of cumulative damage under the same input energy, thus 
providing a “low–damage, non–penetrated” initial state for the subse­
quent aftershock stage.
6.2. Aftershock stage: Buffer layer suppression of cumulative damage and 
performance
The distinctive feature of the aftershock stage is that the lining has 
already sustained mainshock–induced damage, and its dynamic char­
acteristics have been altered. Consequently, even if the peak ground 
acceleration of the aftershock is comparable to that of a low–amplitude 
mainshock, the structural response is significantly amplified. The buffer 
layer exhibits a unique capability to suppress cumulative damage during 
the aftershock stage.
In terms of crack evolution, existing cracks in the unbuffered lining 
propagate rapidly under aftershock loading, become through–going, 
and even extend to the crown. In contrast, cracks in the buffered lining 
extend only slowly along pre–existing paths, and no new penetrating 
cracks appear. This indicates that the buffer layer effectively inhibits 
accelerated crack propagation, delaying the transition from local dam­
age to global failure.
Strain response shows that the unbuffered lining exhibits signifi­
cantly elevated strain levels under aftershock excitation, with further 
enlargement of left–right asymmetry, reflecting cumulative plastic 
deformation and aggravated local failure. The buffered lining, however, 
shows much lower strain levels and better strain symmetry, indicating 
that even if the buffer layer has undergone some deformation during the 
mainshock, it can still maintain its regulation capacity and avoid sec­
ondary stress concentration.
Energy analysis provides the strongest evidence. The Arias intensity 
of the unbuffered lining continues to increase noticeably during the 
aftershock stage, indicating that mainshock–induced damage has 
altered the lining’s dynamic properties, allowing it to absorb additional 
energy even under low energy input. In contrast, the energy curve of the 
buffered lining remains at a low level with slow cumulative growth. The 
difference curve still shows an effective energy dissipation interval 
during the aftershock stage, indicating that the energy regulation 
function of the buffer layer persists.
Damage factor statistics from numerical simulations further quantify 
this trend: after the aftershock, the proportion of severely damaged el­
ements in the unbuffered lining increases by 1.52 %, while that in the 
buffered lining increases by only 0.72 %, and the proportion of slightly 
damaged elements does not increase at all. This fully demonstrates that 
the buffer layer not only inhibits damage initiation during the main­
shock, but more importantly, significantly delays the cumulative evo­
lution of existing damage during the aftershock stage.
The performance enhancement mechanism of the buffer layer in the 
aftershock stage lies in the following: even after the lining has sustained 
mainshock–induced damage, the buffer layer continues to dissipate 
seismic energy and redistribute stresses, suppressing secondary stress 
concentration and accelerated crack propagation, and allowing damage 
to develop in a progressive, controllable manner, thereby avoiding 
sudden through–going failure. This characteristic enables the tunnel 
structure to maintain a longer repairable period and a lower risk of 
collapse under a complete mainshock–aftershock sequence, which is the 
core value of the buffer layer in improving the seismic performance of 
tunnels.

7. Conclusions
Based on a series of large-scale shaking table model tests, this study 
systematically investigated the dynamic response and damage evolution 
of tunnel linings subjected to MS-AS sequences, with particular 
emphasis on the contribution of a buffer layer to the enhancement of 
structural seismic performance. The main conclusions are summarized 
as follows: 
(1) During a strong earthquake event, the mainshock can induce 
initial damage in critical regions of the tunnel lining, such as the 
spandrels and arch springing, characterized predominantly by 
shear-dominated failure. Under subsequent aftershocks, the pre- 
existing damaged zones continue to propagate and gradually 
extend toward the crown, sidewall, and invert, exhibiting pro­
nounced cumulative degradation and delayed coalescence.
(2) During the mainshock stage, the buffer layer considerably re­
duces the global peak acceleration response (by up to 38 %), 
while effectively mitigating local strain concentrations and 
enhancing stress redistribution at critical locations. In the after­
shock stage, it suppresses secondary stress concentrations and 
slows crack propagation, leading to enhanced structural resil­
ience and controlled damage accumulation.
(3) Compared with the case without a buffer layer, the cumulative 
seismic energy input absorbed by the lining system during both 
the mainshock and aftershock stages is significantly reduced 
when a buffer layer is installed, through energy redistribution 
and localized dissipation mechanisms, thereby limiting progres­
sive damage accumulation.
(4) By performing differential analysis of the Arias intensity time 
histories between linings with and without a buffer layer, the 
energy-regulation behavior of the buffer layer can be clearly 
divided into three representative stages: an energy activation 
stage, an effective energy dissipation stage, and an efficiency 
convergence stage. In the early phase of seismic excitation, the 
buffer layer participates only marginally in energy regulation; 
during the dominant energy-input phase, its energy-reduction 
effect rapidly intensifies and becomes the primary factor con­
trolling cumulative damage; and as the seismic energy input di­
minishes, the regulation efficiency gradually converges. This 
stage-dependent behavior demonstrates that the buffer layer 
does not operate with constant efficiency, but rather evolves 
dynamically with the seismic energy level and the structural 
response state.
(5) Parametric analysis identifies the optimal stiffness ratio (≥10 %) 
and thickness ratio (1.25–1.50) of the buffer layer, effectively 
reducing the damage area. Residual damage remains concen­
trated at the arch springing, indicating that the intuitive principle 
of “softer and thicker is always better” should be avoided.
